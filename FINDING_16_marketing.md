# SEVENFOLD / FINDING 16
## "All natural" and "try one free": the deception is provable from the government's own words

**Status:** the contradiction is CONFIRMED from FDA's own assessment. The free-sample
practice is **[S]**, corroborated across independent sources. **Date:** 2026-08-27.

---

### 1. The claim, and the government's own refutation

7-OH products are marketed with the vocabulary of botanicals: **"natural,"
"herbal," "plant based," "wellness."** [S]

FDA's own scientific assessment says otherwise, in plain language:

> "The enhanced amount of 7-OH in these products is **likely synthetically derived
> through oxidate chemical conversion** of mitragynine isolates or kratom extracts.
> Given the trace amounts of 7-OH that are naturally present in kratom, **direct
> extraction of 7-OH from plant material would simply be unfeasible
> economically.**"

**[I] This is not a matter of interpretation.** There is not enough 7-OH in the
plant to sell. Every commercial 7-OH product is made by taking purified
mitragynine and performing a chemical oxidation on it. Two granted US patents
describe exactly how: **US 12,492,201 B1** and **US 12,466,830**, "Method of
converting mitragynine to 7-hydroxymitragynine," claiming yields of >=95% purity
using Fenton's Reagent and oxone monopersulfate.

**A product manufactured by adding a hydroxyl group in a laboratory is being sold
as plant-based.** The industry itself makes this argument, in the other direction,
whenever it wants 7-OH restricted: Sheldon Bradshaw told Ohio legislators that
7-OH is produced by "bad actors" who "expose alkaloids to harsh, toxic agents,"
and called it "a synthetic drug." The leaf sector says it is synthetic when that
helps them. The sellers say it is natural when that helps them. **Both cannot be
right, and FDA has already picked.**

### 2. The free sample is a documented distribution practice

**[S]** Independent reporting describes concentrated 7-OH being given away as
**free samples to existing kratom leaf customers**, including people in long-term
recovery from opioids. Accounts describe leaf consumers of many years accepting a
free sample of concentrated 7-OH and becoming dependent within weeks.

**[I] This is a targeted acquisition strategy aimed at the population least able
to refuse it.** A person who has used leaf kratom for a decade has every reason to
trust a free sample handed over the same counter. The product they are handed is,
per FDA, a mu-opioid agonist more potent than morphine. The transaction is
designed so the first dose costs nothing and the decision to continue is made
after dependence has already begun.

Jay Puckett's own account (Finding 13) follows this pattern exactly, which is
why it is **representative rather than idiosyncratic**.

### 3. Independent regulatory recognition

**Georgia Attorney General Chris Carr, consumer alert, 2025-11-13:** "Dangerous
Synthetic Opioids Flooding Gas Stations." [S]

Note the word **synthetic**, from a state attorney general, describing products
sold as natural.

### 4. Why this is the strongest route that has nothing to do with Mullin

**[I]** Every other line in this case runs through a contested federal rulemaking,
a cabinet official's disclosure, or a lobbying record. This one does not.

- The claim is **"natural."**
- The federal government's own scientific finding is **"synthetically derived."**
- The manufacturing method is **patented**, publicly, with the chemistry described.

That is a deceptive-advertising case, not a drug-policy argument. It sits under
**FTC Act Section 5** and under every state unfair-and-deceptive-practices
statute. It does not require the 7-OH schedule to be finalised, does not require
anyone to prove a conflict of interest, and does not require a single new fact.

It is also the theory that already succeeded once in this sector: *Torres v.
Botanic Tonics*, N.D. Cal. 3:23-cv-01460-VC, settled for **$8,750,000** on the
allegation that a kratom product concealed what it was and what it did.

### 5. Recommended actions

1. **State attorneys general.** Tennessee, and any state where a named product is
   sold. The Georgia alert shows an AG has already reached this conclusion; the
   marketing claim gives them a cause of action rather than a warning.
2. **FTC complaint** under Section 5. Deceptive representation that a
   chemically-synthesised opioid is a natural botanical product.
3. **Preserve the marketing.** Product pages change. Capture the "natural" and
   "plant based" language for named products, with dates and hashes, before the
   scheduling action prompts a rewrite. **This is time sensitive and should be
   done now.**
4. **Document the free-sample practice** through the intake instrument. Add a
   question: *was the first one free, and who gave it to you?* If a pattern shows
   up across respondents, that is an FTC exhibit.
