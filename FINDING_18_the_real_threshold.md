### 2. The operative definition, verbatim

> **(A)** Any botanical material of the plant *Mitragyna speciosa*, also known as
> kratom, and contains more than **0.050 percentage** of 7-hydroxymitragynine on a
> dry weight basis, **or**
>
> **(B)** Any alternative article to that described in (A), that is:
>
>   **i.** Resulting from synthetic methods and containing 7-hydroxymitragynine
>   present in amounts greater than **0.05 percentage** weight/weight,
>   weight/volume, or volume/volume **or greater than 1.00 milligram of
>   7-hydroxymitragynine in the article**, or
>
>   **ii.** Material derived from *Mitragyna speciosa* and **further processed to
>   manufacture alternative dosage forms such as extracts, concentrates, processed
>   edibles, or pressed pills**, and which may have materials that have been
>   exposed to chemical, thermal, or other methods leading to chemical
>   transformations that result in 7-hydroxymitragynine present in amounts greater
>   than **0.05 percentage** ... **or greater than 1.00 milligram of
>   7-hydroxymitragynine in the article**

### 3. What has been missed

**Every account of this rule, including our own filed comment, describes it as a
0.05% threshold.** That is only half of it.

**There is an absolute limit: 1.00 milligram of 7-OH in the article.** For any
product falling under (B), the rule is satisfied by exceeding **either** the
percentage **or** the absolute milligram figure, whichever comes first.

### 4. The arithmetic, against FDA's own numbers

FDA's assessment cites leaf 7-OH content at a **mean of 0.01%**, a **maximum of
0.21%** (Sharma 2025, n=341), and **0.01-0.04%** across commercial products
(Kikura-Hanajiri 2009). It cites typical consumer doses of **1-3 g (49% of users)
and 4-6 g (33%)** (Garcia-Romeu).

| Serving | 1.00 mg is exceeded when 7-OH exceeds |
|---|---|
| 1 g | 0.1000% |
| 3 g | 0.0333% |
| 5 g | **0.0200%** |
| 6 g | **0.0167%** |

**[I] For ordinary serving sizes the absolute limit binds well before the
percentage does.** A 5 g serving crosses 1.00 mg at 0.02% 7-OH, which is inside
the naturally occurring range FDA itself documents, and a fifth of the
percentage threshold.

### 5. The ambiguity, stated precisely

We are careful here because the reading matters and is genuinely unsettled.

- Subsection **(A)**, raw botanical material, carries **only** the percentage test.
  Plain leaf powder is judged on concentration alone.
- Subsection **(B)(ii)** covers material "further processed to manufacture
  alternative dosage forms such as extracts, concentrates, processed edibles, or
  **pressed pills**" and carries **both** tests.
- **(B)(ii) also contains the qualifier "and which may have materials that have
  been exposed to chemical, thermal, or other methods leading to chemical
  transformations."** Whether "may have" is a condition or a description is not
  clear on the face of the text.

**The unresolved question: does an ordinary leaf capsule, tablet or extract, with
no chemical enhancement whatsoever, fall under (B)(ii)?** If it does, the 1.00 mg
absolute limit applies to it, and the arithmetic above puts a substantial share of
ordinary botanical product over the line.

**[I] We do not assert that leaf products are captured. We assert that the text
does not clearly say either way, and that a criminal prohibition should not turn
on that ambiguity.**

### 6. This strengthens what we already filed, and warrants a supplement

Section 2 of our filed comment (`mtc-56yj-nmij`) argued the threshold does not
cleanly separate leaf from concentrate, relying on FDA's report of a 0.21%
maximum in consumer product. **That argument was correct and understated.** We
did not know about the absolute limit when we filed.

HART's extension request (Finding 17) asked precisely this: "how any proposed
threshold should apply across botanical source material, semi-synthetic
derivatives, and finished dosage forms" and "what analytical methodologies are
capable of accurately measuring compliance." **They were right to ask, and the
letter shows why.**

### 7. Two further items from the letter

**a.** HHS states: *"The Food and Drug Administration was asked to review its
files. I have been advised that there are currently **no approved new drug
applications or investigational new drug applications for these substances**."*
As of 2026-03-06, no IND existed for 7-OH. **This does not conflict with the
mitragynine IND reported later in 2026** (Finding 11); the letter concerns 7-OH.
Do not conflate them.

**b.** HHS's formal position is **"no objection"** to DEA's plan. HHS did not
originate the temporary scheduling; DEA notified HHS on 2026-02-24 and HHS
declined to object on 2026-03-06, referencing its own earlier July 2025
recommendation.

**[I] CORRECTED. Our first note on this overstated what the sequence shows.**

The letter establishes which agency **signed** at which point: DEA notified HHS on
2026-02-24 and HHS declined to object on 2026-03-06. **It does not establish who
wanted the action.**

Treating "DEA initiated it" as exculpatory would be a category error in this case
specifically, because the entire architecture here runs through intermediaries.
Botanic Tonics does not lobby as Botanic Tonics. Johnson Foods' science arrives as
an independent journal article. **And a $980,000 vehicle with no disclosed funder
was lobbying DEA and the Department of Justice directly** (Finding 14).

**What the letter supports:** HHS did not originate the temporary scheduling and
its formal role was to decline to object, referencing its own July 2025
recommendation.

**What it does not support:** any claim about whose interests drove the action.
Correct accounts that say HHS drove the scheduling. Do not replace them with an
account that says nobody did.

## ADDENDUM, 2026-08-28: the rule never defines "article", and that is the whole limit

Both limbs of (B) turn on **"greater than 1.00 milligram of 7-hydroxymitragynine
in the article."** The quoted text defines percentage, weight basis and dosage
form. **It does not define "article."**

(B)(ii) expressly contemplates **"pressed pills"** as a covered dosage form. So
the question is unavoidable:

| If "article" means | Effect of the 1.00 mg limit |
|---|---|
| the package as offered for sale | constrains total dose |
| a single pill or capsule within it | **constrains nothing** |

**[I] Under the second reading the limit is trivially evaded.** Forty pressed
pills at 0.9 mg each are individually compliant and deliver **36 mg** in one bag.
The seller changes nothing about the product and changes only the unit.

This is not a theoretical drafting quibble. It is the difference between a rule
that caps exposure and a rule that caps labelling.

**Raised by Jay Puckett on 2026-08-28** while testing whether a per-article
measure could survive contact with the market. It could not, as written.

**No comment on the docket addresses it.** Finding 26 establishes the method for
checking that: single rare words, controlled against a nonsense string.

**Filed** as requested action 4 of the fourth comment, asking the Department to
state whether "article" means the package as offered for sale or an individual
unit within it.
