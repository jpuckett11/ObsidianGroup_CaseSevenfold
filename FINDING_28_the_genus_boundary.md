## A mu-opioid full agonist sits in a supplement sold on open shelves, and the rule's boundary is a genus name

**Status:** CONFIRMED from peer-reviewed primaries.
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-28.
**Opened at the researcher's direction.**

## READ THIS FIRST

**This finding is not a warning about cat's claw and must not be quoted as one.**

We are not saying cat's claw is dangerous. We are not saying anyone should stop
taking it. We have **no** harm signal, **no** adverse event data, and **no**
concentration data for any commercial product. Section 4 sets out, at length, what
this finding does not establish, and Section 4 is the more important half.

What this finding is about is **where the government drew a line, and whether
chemistry respects it.**

**Section 7 answers the concentration question and the answer is reassuring.** An
ordinary cat's claw capsule carries roughly 0.65 mg of the entire tetracyclic
alkaloid class, below the 1.00 mg the government treats as significant for a single
scheduled compound, and no one lobbies on it in either direction. We chased this
hard and published the result that cuts against our own lead.

---

### 1. Corynoxine is a mu-opioid receptor full agonist [P]

*Kratom Alkaloids as Probes for Opioid Receptor Function*, **ACS Chemical
Neuroscience**, 2021, DOI **10.1021/acschemneuro.1c00149**:

> "We identify **corynantheidine** as a mu opioid receptor (MOR) **partial
> agonist**, whereas its oxindole derivative **corynoxine was an MOR full
> agonist**."

> "Corynantheidine, corynoxine, and mitraciliatine showed **MOR dependent
> antinociception in mice**."

Corynantheidine is the parent scaffold of 9-hydroxycorynantheidine, the compound
in Findings 20, 25 and 27. Its **oxindole derivative is a full agonist**.

### 2. Corynoxine is in cat's claw [P]

*Circulating ultrasound-assisted extraction, countercurrent chromatography, and
liquid chromatography*, **Journal of Chromatography A**, 2015, DOI
**10.1016/j.chroma.2015.02.028**:

> "This approach was applied to the fractionation and purification of alkaloids
> from **Uncaria tomentosa**... 9 mg of **rhynchophylline, corynoxine, and
> corynoxine B** with HPLC purities of 96..."

They isolated it from the plant. *Uncaria tomentosa* is **cat's claw**, sold in
the United States as a dietary supplement with no scheduling, no threshold, no
age restriction and no federal concentration limit.

A 2025 pharmacopoeial paper (*Phytochemical Analysis*, DOI 10.1002/pca.70004)
confirms the pattern across the genus: "corynoxine, corynoxine B, rhynchophylline,
and isorhynchophylline dominate in **UM**" (*U. macrophylla*).

### 3. The plants are relatives, and the alkaloid lists overlap heavily

***Mitragyna*** and ***Uncaria*** are both **Rubiaceae**, tribe **Naucleeae**.
They are neighbouring genera and *Uncaria* has historically been classified
alongside *Mitragyna*.

The overlap is visible in an industry document already in this case file.
CaaMTech's kratom composition patent (**WO2021076849A1**, Finding 25) lists as
kratom constituents:

> "...Corynantheidine, **Corynoxine A**, **Corynoxine B**, **Isomitraphylline**,
> **Isorhynchophylline**, **Mitraphylline**, **Rhynchophylline**, Speciophylline,
> ... **Corynoxeine**, **Isopteropodine**, Isorhynchophylline Oxindole..."

**Rhynchophylline, isorhynchophylline, mitraphylline, isomitraphylline,
corynoxeine, isopteropodine and the corynoxines are the standard alkaloid profile
of cat's claw.** A patent describing kratom is largely describing cat's claw's
alkaloids too.

**[I] The alkaloids do not respect the genus boundary. The rule does.**

### 4. What this finding does NOT establish, at length and on purpose

**This is the load-bearing section. Anyone citing Section 1 without Section 4 is
misusing this document.**

- **Corynoxine appears safer than morphine in the way that matters most.** The
  same 2021 paper: mitraciliatine and corynoxine "displayed **attenuated
  respiratory depression** and hyperlocomotion compared to the prototypic MOR
  agonist morphine." Respiratory depression is what kills people in opioid
  overdose. Full agonism at a receptor is not the same as danger to a person.
- **The authors themselves caution against overreading it.** "These minor
  alkaloids are **unlikely to play the majority role** in the biological actions of
  kratom."
- **We have no concentration data for any commercial cat's claw product.** We do
  not know how much corynoxine is in a capsule, and quantity is the entire
  question. The isolation paper reports milligrams recovered in a laboratory
  fractionation, which says nothing about a consumer dose.
- **We have no harm signal.** No adverse event series, no case reports, no
  emergency department pattern, nothing. Cat's claw has been widely consumed for
  decades.
- **We are not calling for cat's claw to be scheduled, restricted, age-gated or
  removed from sale.** We would oppose that on this evidence.
- **We are not asserting anyone is exploiting this.** No product marketing
  reviewed makes any opioid claim for cat's claw, and no evidence suggests anyone
  is selling it for that purpose.

### 5. What it does establish

**The boundary of this rule is a genus name, and the pharmacology is not organised
by genus.**

Trace the line the federal action draws:

| | |
|---|---|
| Covered | 7-OH, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, MGM-16. All mitragynine-scaffold, all from *Mitragyna speciosa* |
| Not covered | 9-hydroxycorynantheidine, a **major human metabolite** of the covered parent (Finding 27) |
| Not covered | Corynoxine, a **mu-opioid full agonist**, present in *Mitragyna* **and** in a supplement genus nobody has looked at |

The action reaches one scaffold in one species. The relevant chemistry spans at
least two scaffolds across at least two genera in the same tribe, and appears in
products regulated under an entirely different framework.

**This is the strongest available demonstration that scope drawn by molecule and
by species cannot hold.** It is the same argument as Finding 27, from a completely
independent direction, and it does not require anyone to be acting in bad faith.

### 6. What we would actually ask

Nothing about cat's claw.

**We would ask the Department to state whether its evaluation considered
mu-opioid-active alkaloids occurring outside *Mitragyna speciosa*, and whether the
scope of this action is intended to be defined by species, by chemical scaffold, or
by pharmacological activity and quantity.**

That question is already before it as requested action 3 of comment
`mtd-holx-p7i5`. This finding is the sharpest illustration of why it matters, and
**it is deliberately not being added to the docket as a claim about a supplement.**

### 7. ANSWERED 2026-08-28: the quantity is small, and there is no money on it

Section 4 named the deciding question as concentration. It has been chased and it
resolves **against** any concern about cat's claw. We publish that as plainly as we
published the finding.

**Quantity [P].** *Phytochemistry* 2018, DOI **10.1016/j.phytochem.2018.03.008**,
*U. tomentosa* from Costa Rica across seasons:

| Plant part | POA : TOA | TOA content |
|---|---|---|
| **Bark and root** (what capsules use) | **6 : 1**, pentacyclic chemotype in all seasons | **1.3 mg/g** |
| Leaf | ~1 : 1 | 3.1 mg/g |

Corynoxine is **one tetracyclic among several**, alongside rhynchophylline,
isorhynchophylline and corynoxeine. It is a fraction of the TOA figure, not the
figure itself.

Applied to a commercial bark capsule:

    400 mg capsule   ->  0.52 mg  ALL tetracyclic alkaloids combined
    500 mg capsule   ->  0.65 mg
    1000 mg capsule  ->  1.30 mg

**For scale: the federal absolute limit for 7-OH is 1.00 mg per article.** An
ordinary cat's claw capsule carries roughly **0.65 mg of the entire tetracyclic
class**, before separating out corynoxine specifically. It sits below the quantity
the government treats as significant for a single scheduled compound.

**And commercial product selects against it.** Bark is a pentacyclic chemotype at
6:1 in every season, the industry markets the pentacyclic fraction, and products
such as Samento are sold specifically as "the pentacyclic chemotype." Corynoxine is
tetracyclic. **The supplement trade has been selecting away from it for commercial
reasons that have nothing to do with opioid pharmacology.**

**Money [P, controlled].** Senate LDA, filings mentioning:

| Term | Filings |
|---|---|
| cat's claw | **0** |
| Uncaria | **0** |
| dietary supplement | 1,910 |
| kratom | 298 |

Controls return results, so the zeros are real. **Nobody lobbies on this.** No
industry is defending it, no industry is attacking it, and there is no money
attached to it in any direction.

### 8. What that means for this finding

**The pharmacology stands. The alarm does not, and we retract any implication of
one.**

Corynoxine is a mu-opioid full agonist and it is in cat's claw. Both remain true.
At the quantities present in ordinary product, with attenuated respiratory
depression relative to morphine, with no harm signal in decades of use, and with
zero commercial or political money anywhere near it, **there is no cat's claw
problem and this case does not claim there is one.**

What survives, and it is the whole point, is the **scope** argument:

A mu-opioid full agonist occurs in a plant the rule does not reach, in a genus the
rule does not name, sold under a regulatory framework the rule does not touch. It
happens to be present in small amounts. **Nothing in the rule's architecture
guarantees that.** The line was drawn at a species and a scaffold, and the only
reason this particular case is harmless is a fact about plant chemistry that the
rule neither knew nor relied on.

**That is the argument. Not the supplement.**
