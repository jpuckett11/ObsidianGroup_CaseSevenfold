# SEVENFOLD / FINDING 34
## Congress did act. It removed the barrier everyone named, and prescribing did not move.

**Status:** CONFIRMED from peer-reviewed evaluations.
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-29.
**This finding answers a promise made in Finding 32**, which stated: *"We do not
claim Congress has done nothing on treatment. It has, over years, and this case has
not surveyed that record."* We have now surveyed part of it.

---

### 1. The record is not empty, and this case will not pretend it is

Congress has legislated repeatedly in this area. The most consequential recent act
is the **Mainstreaming Addiction Treatment (MAT) Act**, which **eliminated the
X-waiver** — the separate DEA registration a clinician previously needed before
prescribing buprenorphine.

That was the single most-cited regulatory barrier in the field for a decade. Advocacy
organisations asked for its removal for years. **Congress removed it.**

**Anyone arguing that Congress has ignored this has to account for that, and this
case accounts for it here.**

### 2. And the evaluations say it did not work

**Am. J. Preventive Medicine, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.amepre.2026.108508.** Comparative
interrupted time series, IQVIA PharMetrics 25% sample, Jan 2021 to Oct 2023:

> "**No differences in either the level** (-8.69 per 10,000; p=0.131) **or trend**
> (+0.74 per month; p=0.380) **of buprenorphine prescribers were found** between
> jurisdiction-months with and without references to the X-waiver after [federal
> elimination]."

> "Findings suggest that removing jurisdiction-level statutory X-waiver references
> is **unlikely to expand access because structural barriers persist**."

**J. Am. Coll. Emergency Physicians Open, 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.acepjo.2025.100246**,
Massachusetts:

> "**There was no change in the total buprenorphine prescriptions per month** ...
> between the pre-no-X-waiver period compared with the post-no-X-waiver period."

### 3. What that actually means, and it is not what it first looks like

**The barrier was never the waiver.**

For a decade the field named a registration requirement as the obstacle. Congress
removed it. Prescribing did not change. The obstacle was somewhere else the whole
time: **capacity, reimbursement, stigma, geography, and the months of care after the
prescription that nobody funds.**

**[I] This reframes the entire question.** The failure is not that Congress refuses
to act. It is that **the action taken was the one that cost nothing.**

Removing a registration requirement is free. It requires no appropriation, no
programme, no staff, and it produces a bill anyone can sign. **Funding prescriber
capacity, coverage past the taper, and the structure that follows treatment costs
money every year forever.**

Congress did the free one. The evaluations say the free one did not work. **The
expensive one has not been tried.**

### 4. This makes Finding 32 sharper, not softer

Finding 32 records that eleven senators wrote 537 words on this subject with **zero**
references to treatment.

Read against this finding, that is no longer a claim that legislators are
indifferent. **It is a claim about which lever they reach for.** Scheduling a
molecule is free. So is repealing a registration requirement. Both were done.

**The intervention with a measured dose-response relationship** — contingency
management, s5 of `REMEDIATION.md` — **costs money in proportion to how well it
works.** It has not been funded at effective levels.

### 5. And the outcome is still going the wrong way

*The Lancet*, Global Burden of Disease 2025, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(25)01917-8:

> "there was an **increase in 70q0 for drug use disorders in the high-income
> super-region**"

**70q0** is the probability of dying before age 70. It is **rising** from drug use
disorders in wealthy countries, against a global trend of falling premature
mortality.

### 6. What this finding does NOT claim

- **Not that the MAT Act was a mistake.** Removing a pointless registration barrier
  was correct. It simply was not sufficient, which is a different thing.
- **Not that Congress has done nothing.** It has done several things. This finding
  names the most significant one and credits it.
- **Not that no federal treatment funding exists.** State Opioid Response grants and
  other appropriations exist and this case has not evaluated their scale.
- **Not that legislators are indifferent.** The evidence supports a narrower and more
  useful claim: **the reforms attempted have been the ones that do not require
  sustained money.**

### 7. What would change our view

Evidence that a federal action requiring **sustained appropriation** — prescriber
capacity, post-taper coverage, contingency management at effective incentive levels —
has been enacted at a scale proportionate to **42.4 million untreated people**.

**We looked and did not find it. If it exists, we will publish it.**
