# SEVENFOLD / FINDING 35
## The cap was real, it bound below the effective dose for two decades, and the agency that was blamed for it said in 2020 that it had never imposed it.

**Status:** CONFIRMED from the governing guidance, the statute, the Federal
Register, a CRS report to Congress, and the underlying clinical trial.
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-28.
**This finding closes the open item flagged in `REMEDIATION.md` section 5**, which
read: *"This case has not verified the current limit and does not assert one... That
is worth someone establishing on the record."* It is now established.

---

## 1. The question

`REMEDIATION.md` established that contingency management (CM) is the one
behavioural intervention for substance use disorder with a demonstrated
dose-response curve: pay more and sooner, it works better; pay less and later, it
stops working. It then flagged, without asserting, that federal anti-kickback rules
had historically capped what CM programmes could pay.

The question was whether that cap bound **below the dose the trials found
effective**. If so, the single intervention with a proven dose-response
relationship was held by regulation below its own effective dose.

**It did bind. For about twenty years. It was substantially loosened in January
2025, seven months before this case opened, and it still binds in three specific
places.**

## 2. What the cap was [P]

Two separate limits were involved, and conflating them is the whole story.

**The OIG limit.** Under the Beneficiary Inducements civil monetary penalty
provision (42 U.S.C. 1320a-7a(a)(5)) and the Anti-Kickback Statute (42 U.S.C.
1320a-7b(b)), HHS OIG issued a **2016 policy statement** saying it would not treat
gifts of "nominal value" as enforceable violations. Nominal value was defined as
**no more than $15 per item and $75 in the aggregate per patient per year.**

**The SAMHSA limit.** SAMHSA wrote those same numbers into its Notices of Funding
Opportunity as a spending rule: **$15 per incentive, $75 per patient per year.**

Congressional Research Service, *Contingency Management for Substance Use
Disorders*, IF12681, Version 2, 21 March 2025 [P]:

> "SAMHSA has historically limited each incentive to a value of $15 (not cash), and
> the total amount per patient to $75 per year."

## 3. What the effective dose is [P]

**The decisive experiment is Petry et al. 2004**, *Addiction* 99(3), DOI
`10.1111/j.1360-0443.2003.00642.x`, PMID 14982548. Its title is literally a
magnitude question: *"Prize reinforcement contingency management for treating
cocaine users: how low can we go, and with whom?"*

120 cocaine outpatients were randomised to three arms for 12 weeks: standard
treatment, standard plus CM at an expected maximum of **$80**, and standard plus CM
at an expected maximum of **$240**. From the abstract:

> "Patients in the $240 CM condition achieved more abstinence than patients in the
> standard condition."

**The $80 arm did not.** A 2024 re-analysis in the *Journal of Substance Use and
Addiction Treatment*, DOI `10.1016/j.josat.2024.209522`, states the comparison in
exactly the terms this finding needs:

> "The lower-cost $80 condition is the closest condition to the current federal $75
> limit that permits a head-to-head comparison of magnitudes."

*("current" is as of that paper's publication in 2024. The limit changed in January
2025; see section 6. We quote it because it establishes that researchers in the field
read the $75 cap as the operative constraint right up to the month it was lifted.)*

It found effects favouring $240 over $80 of **d = 0.39 to 0.83**.

Corroborating ranges, all [P]:

| Source | Effective magnitude |
|---|---|
| SAMHSA Advisory PEP24-06-001, prize-based | **$250–$450** per patient over 12 weeks |
| SAMHSA Advisory PEP24-06-001, escalating voucher | **$250 to almost $1,000** over 12 weeks |
| CRS IF12681 | under **$100** is "low"; over **$1,000** is "high" |
| Two AI/AN culturally adapted RCTs | **$667** and **$803** |

**So the cap sat at $75 per year against a floor of roughly $240–$250 per
twelve-week course.** The one arm ever run at a magnitude close to the cap was
tested against usual care and did not beat it.

## 4. The part that costs almost nothing, and matters most [P]

Petry 2004 also reports what patients **actually earned**:

> "On average, patients in the two CM conditions earned $36 and $68 in prizes."

The arm that worked cost, on average, **$68 per patient**. That is *below the old
$75 cap.* The cap did not bind on what was spent. It bound on **what could be
won** — the ceiling, the size of the possible prize, which is the therapeutically
active variable. SAMHSA says the same thing about its new limit:

> "SAMHSA has set the total per-patient CM incentive value limit at $750 per year,
> with no per incentive cap... **Research suggests that very few patients will reach
> this limit.**"

**This is the single most useful number in this finding.** Raising a cap from $75 to
$750 does not multiply programme cost by ten, because the cap governs a ceiling that
most patients never touch. The old limit was suppressing the efficacy of the
intervention at almost no fiscal saving. It was close to free to be wrong.

## 5. The twist: OIG says it never imposed the $75 cap [P]

In the preamble to its **2020 final rule**, 85 Fed. Reg. 77,684 at 77,791-92 (2 Dec
2020), OIG addressed CM directly. Per CRS IF12681:

> "It also clarified that the agency's 2016 policy statement concerning gifts of
> nominal value **does not impose a $75 annual limit on CM incentives.** Additionally,
> OIG stated that the agency would examine CM programs that do not meet the patient
> engagement and support safe harbor (or another safe harbor) on a case-by-case
> basis."

SAMHSA's own January 2025 advisory concedes the same history:

> "the **belief** that certain guidance published by the U.S. Department of Health
> and Human Services (HHS) Office of Inspector General (OIG) imposed a CM incentive
> value cap of $75 per patient, per year. HHS-OIG has clarified its views on this
> point in guidance."

**So from December 2020 to January 2025 — four years — the binding constraint on the
only dose-responsive treatment for stimulant use disorder was a reading of a policy
statement that the issuing agency had publicly disclaimed.** SAMHSA kept $75 in its
funding notices anyway. Nobody had to be lobbied. It was inertia sitting on top of a
correction nobody applied.

## 6. What changed, and when [P]

**SAMHSA Advisory PEP24-06-001, *Using SAMHSA Funds to Implement Evidence-Based
Contingency Management Services*, January 2025:**

> "SAMHSA now allows those recipients of a SAMHSA grant that authorizes
> SAMHSA-approved CM activities in treating SUDs, to provide a motivational incentive
> value of **up to $750 per patient, per year**"

Non-cash only: items, vouchers, or gift cards. Cash is not permitted.

**$750 is inside the effective range.** For the first time, a federal funding stream
permits CM at a magnitude the trials support.

## 7. Where it still binds [P]

Three places. None of them are rhetorical.

**a. $750 is above the safe harbor, so it is unprotected.** The patient engagement
and support safe harbor created by the 2020 rule (42 C.F.R. 1001.952(hh)) carries
an inflation-adjusted annual cap: **$605 for 2025, $623 for 2026** (OIG annual
inflation updates). SAMHSA's own footnote concedes the gap:

> "Although the highest allowed value permitted by this guidance — $750 per patient,
> per year — would fall outside the safe harbor specified by the current regulation
> ($605 per patient, per year for 2025) ... the preamble of the regulation clarifies
> that 'if a contingency management incentive that implicates the Federal
> antikickback statute, Beneficiary Inducements CMP, or both does not satisfy an
> existing safe harbor or exception (as applicable), that does not mean that such
> incentive automatically violates the statutes and is illegal.'"

Read that carefully. It is not a grant of protection. It is a statement that being
outside the safe harbor is **not automatically illegal**, which leaves providers
with case-by-case enforcement exposure. A clinic is being told to rely on
prosecutorial discretion.

**b. The safe harbor excludes the delivery method SAMHSA requires.** Per CRS, OIG
"declined to extend the safe harbor to include explicit protection for cash,
cash-equivalent payments, or **gift cards**, due to concerns over a 'heightened fraud
and abuse risk.'" SAMHSA's advisory tells grantees to use vouchers or gift cards. So
CM as SAMHSA instructs it to be delivered may fall outside that safe harbor **at any
dollar value**, not merely above $623.

**c. It does not reach Medicaid.** The $750 applies to State Opioid Response and
Tribal Opioid Response grants. CRS: *"Most states report that Medicaid is their
largest public funder of SUD treatment."* California's Medicaid pilot pays a maximum
of **$599 per year** — set, as CRS notes, "just below the Internal Revenue Service's
$600 threshold" for 1099 reporting. **The ceiling on the largest funder is drawn by a
tax-reporting boundary, not by a clinical one.**

## 8. Congress ordered a fix. It has not been delivered. [P]

The **Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023** (P.L. 117-328) directed OIG to review
whether to establish a safe harbor for "evidence-based contingency management
incentives" and "the parameters for such a safe harbor," and to report
recommendations to Congress **by 29 December 2024**. In a November 2023 report, HHS
said consideration was ongoing.

OIG opened **RIN 0936-AA13** for precisely this, titled *Medicare and State Health
Care Programs: Fraud and Abuse; Establishment of New Safe Harbors Under the Federal
Anti-Kickback Statute*, covering "evidence-based contingency management programs for
the treatment of substance use disorders." The Unified Agenda scheduled the Notice of
Proposed Rulemaking for **August 2024**.

**As of 2026-08-28, RIN 0936-AA13 has never published a document in the Federal
Register.** Queried against the Federal Register API by RIN: 0 documents.

**Control, because a negative without one is worthless** (see `FINDINGS.md` on
provenance discipline). The identical query shape returns:

| RIN | Documents | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **0936-AA13** | **0** | the CM safe harbor |
| 0936-AA10 | 3 | includes the 2020 final rule at 85 FR 77684 |
| 0936-AA15 | 1 | published 2026-01-29 |
| 0936-AA16 | 1 | published 2026-06-24 |

The instrument works. The rule is absent.

**The statutory reporting deadline passed 20 months ago. The proposed rule is 24
months past its own target date.** OIG has meanwhile published two other
anti-kickback documents, in January and June 2026.

## 9. What this finding does and does not say

**It says:** a federal cap held the one dose-responsive SUD treatment below its
effective dose for roughly two decades; for the last four of those years the cap
rested on a reading of guidance the issuing agency had disclaimed; the cost of the
error was minimal because the cap governed a ceiling few patients reach; it was
raised in January 2025 to a level the evidence supports, for one funding stream; and
the safe harbor Congress ordered has not issued.

**It does not say** anyone acted corruptly. There is no evidence of that and this
case does not allege it. It also does not say the cap explains the treatment gap in
`REMEDIATION.md` section 4 — CM is one intervention for one part of one population,
and 42.4 million people are receiving nothing for reasons overwhelmingly unrelated to
this rule.

**It does say** that when this case asked what a serious federal remediation effort
would look like, the first place it checked contained a twenty-year-old regulatory
error that suppressed an effective treatment, cost almost nothing to make, took four
years to correct after the correction was available, and is still only half fixed.

## 10. What would change this finding

- A Federal Register document under RIN 0936-AA13 would moot section 8.
- An OIG report to Congress under P.L. 117-328 that we have not located. We searched
  the Federal Register and OIG's site; we did not obtain a negative confirmation from
  OIG itself. **This is the weakest link in the finding and is labelled as such.**
- Evidence that the $750 limit has been extended beyond SOR/TOR, or that Medicaid
  guidance has moved off the $599 tax boundary.
- A trial showing efficacy at or below $75 per year, which would invert section 3.
