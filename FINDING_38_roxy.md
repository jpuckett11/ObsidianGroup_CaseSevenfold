## 1. The document [P]

***Botanic Tonics LLC v. Kent Leonhardt***, No. **2:25-cv-00680** (S.D. W. Va.,
Charleston Division), **Complaint filed 19 November 2025**, 21 pages with ten
exhibits. Judge Joseph R. Goodwin. Counsel of record includes **Jonathan W. Emord**,
appearing as visiting attorney.

**Kent Leonhardt is West Virginia's Commissioner of Agriculture. He is sued in his
INDIVIDUAL CAPACITY**, which the complaint states in terms:

> "against **Kent Leonhardt, Commissioner of the West Virginia Department of
> Agriculture ("Defendant"), in his individual capacity**, for acting under color of
> state law to violate BT's rights under the U.S. Constitution."

The claims are **Dormant Commerce Clause**, **Supremacy Clause**, and **Equal
Protection and Due Process** under the Fourteenth Amendment, brought through **42
U.S.C. § 1983**, seeking **declaratory judgment and compensatory damages**.

**A state agriculture commissioner was sued personally for money damages for
declining to register a product.** Whatever the merits, that is a posture with
consequences for every regulator who reads about it.

## 3. Why this matters, and exactly how far it goes

**This is the first named, dated, brand-and-dose-specific list of 7-OH products in
this case that comes from a document filed under oath in federal court.** Finding 30
established that these products remain on shelves. This is a filing that says a
**state licensed them**, and names them.

**The provenance limit is important and is not a technicality.** Botanic Tonics is a
commercial rival of the products it lists. It made this statement to win a lawsuit
against the regulator that excluded it. **The complaint is primary evidence of what
BT alleged. It is not primary evidence that West Virginia registered these products.**
That is checkable against the WVDA product registry and has not been checked.

**We are recording an adverse party's assertion because it is specific enough to
falsify.** That is the only reason it is here.

## 4. The dose figures, and what we are NOT claiming

The listed products carry milligram figures: **128 mg, 20 mg, 16 mg.**

**We do not claim these are 7-OH quantities.** Several are labeled "**Complex
Alkaloids**," which ordinarily denotes total alkaloid content rather than a single
compound. The complaint asserts the products "include 7-hydroxymitragynine synthetic
concentrates" without stating the 7-OH fraction.

**This matters because Finding 18 established a federal absolute limit of 1.00 mg per
article.** If any part of a 128 mg figure were 7-OH, the relationship to that limit
would be dramatic. **We are not going to assert that on a product name.** Resolving it
requires a certificate of analysis or the registration filing, and either would
convert this from an allegation into a finding.

## 5. What West Virginia's stated reason was [S, adverse party]

Per the complaint, WVDA refused *feel free* on the ground that combination kratom and
kava products are **not "Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) in accordance with 21 CFR
Part 170.30"** and are unapproved food additives.

**Note the symmetry with Finding 37.** Utah reached *feel free* because it is a
**combination product**. West Virginia refused it because it is a **combination
product**. Two states, two mechanisms, the same operative fact, and in both the
company sued.

**The West Virginia Department of Agriculture maintains a product registry.** If it is
public, the ten product names above either appear in it or they do not. That single
lookup converts §2 from an adverse party's assertion into either a finding or a
correction, and it costs nothing.
