# The Threshold

### How a defensible drug rule got made by the people it pays

**Obsidian Watch Group** · CASE SEVENFOLD · draft of 2026-08-27
**Status: INTERNAL. Not cleared for publication.**

## What this is

A Secretary of Homeland Security owns between $500,001 and $1,000,000 of a kratom
company. Before he ran that department, as a senator, he stood at a podium with
the Health Secretary and the FDA Commissioner and called for federal action
against a competing kratom product. That action is now most of the way through
becoming law. It does not touch the category he is invested in.

That is the story as it has been reported. We went looking to see whether it held
up. Most of it does. One important part of it does not, and we think the part that
does not hold up has been distracting everyone from something worse.

Everything below is sourced. Where we are inferring, we say so. Where we were
wrong earlier in this investigation, we say that too, because we were.

## The short version

Two things are true at the same time, and people keep insisting it has to be one
or the other.

**The rule is probably right.** 7-OH is a genuinely dangerous compound. It is
roughly what you would get if you took the mild thing in the plant and industrially
converted it into something with morphine's profile. There are US patents on how
to do that conversion. The 0.05% line separating it from ordinary leaf comes out of
published chemistry going back to 2009. A University of Florida pharmacologist
tested one of these products for a newspaper in 2023 and called it legal morphine,
eighteen months before any of the lobbying we traced. We spent a lot of this
investigation trying to prove that threshold was reverse engineered to protect
somebody's business, and we could not, because it was not.

**The process that produced it was bought.** The company that stands to gain moved
about $2.9 million through political committees and lobbying firms. Its founder is
a securities fraudster operating under a changed name. The Health Secretary's
aligned PAC took a million dollars from that company in two payments. The evidence
base FDA relied on includes a human trial paid for by another kratom company that
lobbies the federal government, written entirely by that company's paid consultants,
one of whom is chief scientist at the first company. And a cabinet secretary sat on
an undisclosed stake in the beneficiary the whole way through.

**And there is a third thing nobody is looking at.** While all of this was
happening, the same official pressed the health department to delete consumer
warnings about the products he owns. Those products are the ones with the actual
body count. By the end of 2025, FDA had removed the links.

## What we can prove

### The stake was never on the list

Mullin's ethics agreement, signed 2026-03-17, made him divest 63 stocks and 19
municipal bonds within 90 days of confirmation. Apple. Microsoft. Nvidia. Amazon.
Alphabet. Goldman Sachs. RTX. L3Harris. Philip Morris International.

He did it. Two transaction reports filed 2026-07-23 record 98 sales working
through the list.

Botanic Tonics LLC is on neither attachment. The word "botanic" does not appear
anywhere in the ethics agreement. It appears once across all five of his federal
ethics filings: line 25 of his nominee disclosure, "$500,001 - $1,000,000."

He then certified that he had completed every divestiture his ethics agreement
required. As far as we can tell, that certification is accurate. The holding was
never on the list to begin with.

The agreement shows the mechanism for keeping something: for another company,
Apres Spectrum LLC, it says in plain language that the agency determined
divestiture was not necessary because recusal would handle the conflict. That
sentence exists in the document. It is not there for Botanic Tonics. The company
is simply absent.

### It appears in no congressional filing at all

We pulled and text-searched 28 of his filings: House disclosures for 2019, 2020 and
2021, Senate annual reports for 2022, 2023, 2023 twice more as amended, and 2024,
and all 23 Senate periodic transaction reports from January 2025 through March 2026.

Botanic Tonics is in none of them. We ran a control on every single file first, so
we know the search worked and the documents loaded.

There is a structural reason. His calendar year 2025 annual report, the one that
would have covered a 2025 purchase, was extended on 2026-03-16 and never filed. He
was confirmed to DHS seven days later. And the nominee form that did reveal the
holding has a transactions section that reads, in full: "Not required for this type
of report."

So we cannot tell you when he bought it. Nobody can, from public records. The
window is 2025-01-01 to 2026-03-10 and the July 2025 press conference sits inside
it. Anyone telling you he definitely owned it that day is going past the evidence.

### The offering

There is one more document, and as far as we can tell nobody has pulled it.

Botanic Tonics has an SEC registration. CIK 0002041420, a Delaware LLC formed in
2020, run out of Calabasas, California. Broken Arrow is the factory, not the
company. It has filed exactly twice, and both filings are the same private
placement.

A Form D signed 2024-11-21 opens a $25,000,000 raise, first sale dated 2024-11-07,
$2,427,676 in from 28 accredited investors. An amendment signed 2025-01-07 closes
it: $25,000,000 sold, nothing remaining, 30 investors. Two people put in
$22,572,324 between those dates. It is signed by Cameron Korehbandi as chief
executive, and it lists Jerry Ross as a director, Korehbandi as an officer, and a
third officer, Sandro Starna, the company's chief financial officer, who does not
appear in any of the reporting on this story.

That is the only registered offering this company has ever made. There is no 2025
round and no 2026 round. So if Mullin came in through it, he came in between
2024-11-07 and 2025-01-07.

Which is where it gets interesting. An interest held on 31 December 2024 belongs on
his calendar year 2024 annual report, and that report was filed on 13 August 2025
and does not mention the company. An interest bought in the first week of January
2025 belongs on the calendar year 2025 annual, and that one was never filed.

We want to be careful here, because we cannot put him in that round. No document
does. What we can say is that the company's only offering closed in the first week
of January 2025, that a purchase in that week would fall into the single reporting
period that went missing, and that his stake is small enough to be the junior half
of the two investments that closed the round.

We also want to retract something. Earlier in this investigation we treated the
absence of Botanic Tonics from his periodic transaction reports as possible
evidence of a reporting failure. We then parsed all 43 of those reports, from
October 2023 to March 2026. Every transaction in every one of them is a listed
stock, an ETF, a fund or a municipal bond. He holds at least a dozen private
companies and not one of them has ever appeared in a periodic report either. His
office does not treat private interests as reportable that way. The silence there
is ordinary, and we were wrong to weigh it.

Its certification programme costs member companies $5,000 a year. Two auditors told

## Where we were wrong

We started from the idea that the 7-OH ban was manufactured to protect a
politician's investment. We tested that hard and it failed, three separate ways.

FDA's own scientific assessment derives the 0.05% figure from measurements of
natural 7-OH in kratom leaf published in 2009 and 2019, plus a 341-sample study of
consumers' own products. The line sits at the ceiling of what the plant actually
makes. There are granted US patents describing exactly how to manufacture 7-OH from
bulk mitragynine, which confirms these are factory products and not botanicals.
And the researcher whose data the threshold rests on independently called one of
these products legal morphine, to a newspaper, in December 2023.

If we published the strong version, a competent opponent would take it apart in one
paragraph using FDA's own citations, and everything else here would go down with it.

We also got a number wrong ourselves. We first put MIT45 on the 7-OH side of the
spending split, which made the asymmetry look like 8.6 to 1. MIT45 is an
association-endorsed brand selling high-potency mitragynine extract. Corrected, the
asymmetry is about 487 to 1. The correction is in the file with the original
reasoning kept underneath it.

And one more, which matters for anything written from here: **7-OH is not banned.**
As of today it is a proposed rule. The only thing that actually became Schedule I
is three related derivatives, finalised on 2026-08-26.

## The part with no defence

Here is what we think the story actually is.

Restricting a more dangerous competitor is coherent public health policy whatever
the motive behind it. You can hold your nose about the process and still think the
outcome is correct. We do.

But the same official who fronted that action also pressed the health department to
remove kratom harm warnings from the FDA website. By the end of 2025 the links were
gone. FDA refused to delete a warning about liver toxicity, seizures and substance
use disorder, and that warning is still on the page today, so somebody inside held
the line. But the pressure was real and it partly worked.

The products those warnings covered are leaf and mitragynine products. That is the
category he owns. It is also the category with the deaths. The Tampa Bay Times
counted more than 580 kratom-involved overdose deaths in Florida alone over a
decade, 46 of them from kratom by itself, with blood levels in individual cases at
2,500, 4,300 and 7,182 nanograms per millilitre. Public Citizen counts 26 deaths
across five years where a coroner named mitragynine specifically.

Mitragynine is not scheduled. There is no pending action against it. Its warnings
were the ones somebody tried to delete.

There is no scientific literature that supports removing a consumer warning. There
is no expert who will defend it. Unlike the threshold, which survives scrutiny, this
does not survive being said out loud.

## Who is actually getting hurt

We went looking for the people at the end of all this, in the government's own
records rather than in anybody's press release.

FDA runs an adverse event reporting system for foods and supplements. It is
voluntary and unverified, and a death recorded in it is a death somebody described,
not a death anyone proved. That caveat has to travel with every number here. With
it attached: **879 reports name kratom products.** Two hundred and seventy six of
them record a death. Two hundred and thirty nine record a hospitalisation. One
hundred and thirty five record a disability.

The single most reported effect, after death itself, is **dependence**, at 256. Then
withdrawal syndrome at 98. Then overdose, convulsions, seizures.

The people in these reports are not who the marketing suggests. The largest age
group is **25 to 34**, with 278 reports. Then 35 to 44, with 220. Eleven reports
involve someone under eighteen.

Reports are rising. There were 151 last year, the second worst year on record.

**Sixty two of those reports name Feel Free.** We checked the brand strings one by
one, and they are unmistakable: Botanic Tonics Feel Free Classic, kava root and
leaf kratom, in a little blue bottle. Those 62 reports include four deaths, 19
hospitalisations, 16 disabilities and 11 life-threatening events.

The most reported effect for Feel Free is dependence, at 36, followed by withdrawal
syndrome at 17.

Sit with that for a second. The dominant recorded harm from the product a sitting
cabinet secretary owns between half a million and a million dollars of is
**addiction**. Addiction is what the class action said the company hid. Addiction is
what the industry's chief lobbyist has spent years denying. And addiction warnings
are the category somebody leaned on the FDA to take off its website.

O.P.M.S., the brand at the centre of the supply chain investigation, accounts for
five deaths and ten hospitalisations. Products naming 7-OH account for seven deaths
and thirty hospitalisations. Nobody in this fight has clean hands.

There are also 83 federal recall records involving kratom, including multiple Class
I recalls, the most serious kind, from the 2018 salmonella outbreak.

## One thing that argues against us

We should say this plainly because it is the strongest answer to our own argument.

We have made a lot of the fact that mitragynine, the alkaloid with the body count,
is not being scheduled while its competitor is. There is an innocent explanation and
it is a good one.

Mitragynine now has Investigational New Drug status. NIDA is running the first human
trial of a purified mitragynine formulation, thirty two volunteers, starting this
September, aimed at treating opioid use disorder. Putting mitragynine in Schedule I
would obstruct a federal programme trying to treat addiction.

That is a real reason, it has nothing to do with anyone's investments, and anybody
writing about this should say so.

It does not rescue the other half. A compound in Phase 1 safety testing is not a
proven-safe consumer product sitting in a gas station cooler. If anything, an active
drug trial is a reason to warn people more clearly about the unregulated version,
not to quietly delete the warnings.

We will also say, because fairness runs both ways, that the researchers whose work
underpins the threshold are federally funded. The Florida group holds about $4.9
million in NIH grants across nine projects. Their science is not bought. The
industry money we found sits in one specific paper, and it should not be smeared
across the field.

## The asymmetry of exit

There is one argument in this file that survives every concession we have made,
and it is the one worth carrying out of here.

The investigator's own case is close to a best case. He knew it was an opioid
before he took the first tablet. He chose the moment and the product. He had a
physician who would see him, money for the prescription, a house to be ill in and
another adult in it, and he intended from the start to stop and to write it down.

With all of that, it took three weeks, six thousand milligrams, and a course of
prescribed buprenorphine to get out. Physical dependence was established inside
seven days in a man who had never been dependent on anything.

Now take the advantages away, one at a time, and you have the ordinary purchaser.

He does not know it is an opioid, because the label says botanical supplementation.
He does not know a serving is a quarter of the tablet he just swallowed whole. He
has not read the sentence conceding it is habit-forming, because that sentence is
in a help-centre article and he was on the product page. He has no appointment
this month and no coverage if he did. There is no spouse coming home. And when it
turns, he has no framework for what is happening to him and exactly one
intervention available at any hour: buy more.

That is not a person with less character. It is the same trap with the exits
removed, and every removal was a decision somebody made in a meeting.

The harm lands, in the government's own reporting, on people aged twenty-five to
forty-four, with dependence as the most reported effect after death, and eleven
reports involving someone under eighteen.

Meanwhile seven organised vehicles and $7.4 million represented the sellers of one
product class, one vehicle and $15,000 represented the sellers of the other, and
nobody at all represented the people becoming dependent. The only consumer-facing
safety information in the whole affair was federal warning text on an FDA web
page, and that is the one thing that got removed.

We are not going to tell you what any official was thinking. We do not know, and
the record does not need it. We will tell you what the documents show: the man who
stood at the podium holds up to a million dollars of the category that stays on
the shelf, that holding appears on no divestiture list and in no congressional
filing, he pressed to have the warnings taken down, and they came down.

## Where the product comes from

Over 90% of the world's kratom comes from Indonesia, mostly West Kalimantan on
Borneo and Sumatra. In some villages in Kapuas Hulu, eight in ten residents harvest
it. Roughly 250,000 Indonesian farmers are involved.

7-OH is barely in the plant. There is not enough to extract economically. So
producers buy purified mitragynine by the kilogram and add a hydroxyl group in a
lab. Both sides of this commercial war run on the same Indonesian leaf. The fight
is over which downstream chemistry is legal.

The Times traced the leaf route: Pontianak, then Oakland which takes about half of
all imports and Tampa which takes 14%, then a warehouse outside Atlanta, then
extraction in Colorado or sterilisation in Texas, then processing at two unmarked
Georgia buildings known internally as 1100 and 1199, then packaging in Dallas, then
Florida gas stations. Fifteen thousand miles. Four thousand tons a month.

Along that route, according to the Times: imports declared as fertilizer, kelp,
seaweed and potpourri. Blind bills of lading. Shipping labels cut off and destroyed
separately. Product referred to as flour, tea, vitamins, or Product B. A staff
training document coaching employees on what to tell FDA, under the heading Less is
MORE. A state inspector in Colorado told the facility handled a different plant
entirely. A trucking company hauling it through Alabama, where it is illegal,
believing it was carrying vitamins.

The man at the centre of that network is Peyton Palaio. His family trust owns
Johnson Foods LLC. Johnson Foods paid for the human trial that FDA cites in the
scientific assessment justifying all of this, and it lobbies the federal government
for $130,000 a year through Holland & Knight.

That is the loop. The supply chain and the science are the same people.
