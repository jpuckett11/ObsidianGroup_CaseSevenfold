SUMMARY

We request that the Commission investigate the marketing of concentrated
7-hydroxymitragynine products, which are sold to consumers as natural botanical
supplements while the federal government's own scientific assessment finds them
to be synthetically produced by chemical conversion.

The sellers know. On at least one seller's own website, the product page
advertises "botanical supplementation" while a separate help-center page on the
same domain concedes the products "can be habit-forming" and that "long-term use
may lead to addiction and withdrawal symptoms."

Obsidian Watch Group is an independent investigative organization. We have no
financial interest in kratom, 7-OH, or any company named here, and no funding
from any party. We are not seeking damages and represent no claimant.

---

1. THE PRODUCT

7-hydroxymitragynine is an opioid. FDA states it "demonstrates substantially
greater mu-opioid receptor potency" than morphine and "produces respiratory
depression, physical dependence, and withdrawal symptoms characteristic of
classical opioids, such as morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, and hydrocodone."

The specific product examined here, Limitless Black Edition 7-Hydroxymitragynine
Tablets, is labeled at 50 mg of 7-OH per tablet, 500 mg per ten-count bottle,
with a labeled serving size of one quarter tablet, that is 12.5 mg.

2. THE REPRESENTATION IS FALSE ON THE GOVERNMENT'S OWN FINDING

The products are marketed with the vocabulary of botanicals. From the captured
product pages:

  "Experience the ultimate in botanical supplementation with LIMITLESS BLACK
   EDITION 7-Hydroxymitragynine Tablets."
   -- 7ohplus.com, captured 2026-08-27
      sha256 784b9fa6fcf89b06020a738622879789fe1b7d6843e95428add286e9fddec6fc

  "a powerful botanical extract" ... "masking the natural bitterness of alkaloids"
   -- pureleafkratom.com, captured 2026-08-27
      sha256 803bc090846b44983509e228c5e054640dbc61863fd3eb770c00f1f22a22e913

FDA's assessment, "7-Hydroxymitragynine (7-OH): An Assessment of the Scientific
Data and Toxicological Concerns Around an Emerging Opioid Threat," states:

  "The enhanced amount of 7-OH in these products is likely synthetically derived
   through oxidate chemical conversion of mitragynine isolates or kratom
   extracts. Given the trace amounts of 7-OH that are naturally present in
   kratom, direct extraction of 7-OH from plant material would simply be
   unfeasible economically."

The manufacturing route is not in dispute. It is patented. United States Patents
12,492,201 B1 and 12,466,830, both titled "Method of converting mitragynine to
7-hydroxymitragynine," describe oxidation processes using Fenton's Reagent and
oxone monopersulfate yielding at least 95 percent purity.

The industry itself characterizes these products as synthetic when it suits a
different purpose. Sheldon Bradshaw, formerly FDA Chief Counsel, testifying for
the trade group Botanicals for Better Health and Wellness before Ohio
legislators, described 7-OH as "a synthetic drug" produced by "bad actors" who
expose alkaloids to "harsh, toxic agents."

A compound manufactured by performing a patented chemical oxidation on a purified
isolate is not a botanical supplement, and the parties selling it are aware of
that.

3. THE SELLERS DISCLOSE THE RISK WHERE BUYERS WILL NOT SEE IT

On 7ohplus.com, the product page carries the "botanical supplementation" language
quoted above. A help-center entry on the same domain, under the heading "Is 7Oh
safe?", states:

  "While 7OH and kratom are often considered safe for healthy adults when used
   responsibly, it's essential to understand the potential risks. Kratom contains
   compounds that can be habit-forming. Long-term use may lead to addiction and
   withdrawal symptoms."

Both were captured from the same domain on the same day and are preserved with
hashes. The seller possesses the material information and places it away from the
point of purchase.

We submit that this is a material omission under Section 5. A reasonable consumer
choosing among wellness products would consider a risk of addiction and
withdrawal material to the decision, and would not expect to locate it in a
help-center article.

4. DOSAGE PRESENTATION

The unit sold is a 50 mg tablet. The labeled serving is one quarter of that
tablet, 12.5 mg. The product is dispensed, priced and consumed as tablets. Where
a single unit contains four labeled servings of an opioid more potent than
morphine, the Commission may wish to consider whether the serving-size
presentation is likely to mislead.

5. FREE SAMPLE DISTRIBUTION

Independent reporting describes concentrated 7-OH being distributed as free
samples to existing purchasers of leaf kratom, including persons in recovery from
opioid use, with dependence following within weeks. We have not independently
verified this practice and flag it as reported rather than established. If
substantiated, a practice of providing an opioid at no cost to a population in
recovery, at the point of sale of a different product, warrants the Commission's
attention on its own.

6. CONSUMER HARM IN THE GOVERNMENT'S OWN RECORDS

FDA's CFSAN Adverse Event Reporting System contains 879 reports naming kratom
products. CAERS is voluntary, unverified, and does not establish causation. With
that stated: 276 reports record a death, 239 a hospitalization, 135 a disability.
The most frequently reported reaction after death is dependence, at 256 reports,
followed by withdrawal syndrome at 98.

Four reports name Limitless 7-OH products specifically, two of them Black Edition.
Recorded outcomes include three "Other Serious or Important Medical Event" and one
Disability. Reported reactions include withdrawal syndrome, restless legs
syndrome, hyperhidrosis, insomnia, anxiety and dehydration. Ages reported: 23, 37,
56, 69.

We note that FDA issued warning letters to seven firms marketing 7-OH products on
15 July 2025. Limitless was not among them.

7. WHO THE HARM FALLS ON

The Commission may wish to note where the removals land.

A purchaser who knows the product is an opioid, knows the serving size, has a
physician, can afford treatment and has stable housing can extricate himself, with
difficulty. We have documented one such case: three weeks, 6,000 mg, and a
prescribed course of buprenorphine.

A purchaser who has been told the product is botanical supplementation, who has
not seen the habit-forming disclosure because it sits on a different page, who has
no available appointment and no coverage, and who does not recognise opioid
withdrawal when it begins, has one immediately available intervention. It is to
buy more of the product.

Every element of that difference is a marketing decision rather than a
pharmacological one. The compound is the same. What changes is what the buyer was
told, and where.

8. PRECEDENT IN THIS SECTOR

The theory has already succeeded once. In re Botanic Tonics Litigation, No.
3:23-cv-01460-VC (N.D. Cal.), settled for $8,750,000 on allegations that a
kratom product was marketed without disclosing its kratom content or its
addictive potential.

---

REQUESTED ACTION

1. Investigate whether describing chemically converted 7-hydroxymitragynine as
   "botanical supplementation," "botanical extract" or equivalent constitutes a
   deceptive representation under Section 5.

2. Investigate whether placing addiction and withdrawal disclosures outside the
   point of purchase, while the product page carries wellness framing,
   constitutes a material omission.

3. Investigate the reported practice of distributing free 7-OH samples to
   purchasers of other kratom products.

4. Consider coordination with FDA, which has issued warning letters in this
   category, and with state attorneys general. The Georgia Attorney General
   issued a consumer alert on 13 November 2025 describing these products as
   "Dangerous Synthetic Opioids Flooding Gas Stations."
