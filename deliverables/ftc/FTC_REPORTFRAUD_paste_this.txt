I am reporting deceptive marketing of opioid-active products sold as botanical energy supplements. The strongest example is a brand where the seller states its own method in writing.

THE SELLER EXPLAINS THE METHOD ON ITS OWN HOMEPAGE

Brand: "Buzzers," sold nationally, with a companion line "Buzzin with The Homiez." Pages captured and SHA-256 hashed 28 August 2026.

getbuzzers.com carries the banner "No 7-OH or Kratom. Legal in all 50 states," and states:

"After much research, we figured out a way to extract an indole alkaloid from the Cat's Claw plant to mirror the effects of popular kratom products. We're able to get so close because, despite not using kratom, we're still producing a very similar indole alkaloid. The results are a non-kratom product with kratom-like effects."

A retailer page (thegreendragoncbd.com) states the rationale outright: "Legal in all 50 states: Cat's Claw is a recognized botanical and is not a controlled substance, so Buzzers ship nationwide."

IT IS MARKETED AS AN ENERGY SUPPLEMENT

The copy promises "clarity, focus, and high-functioning energy" and "a balanced and productive vibe." The manufacturer's homepage carries a testimonial reading "if you like kratom you'll love these. They give you energy."

A STATE POISON CONTROL CENTER SAYS IT IS AN OPIOID

Utah Poison Control Center, 22 July 2026, naming "Buzzers" and "Homiez" brands: these products "may lead to life-threatening opioid overdose, dependence, or withdrawal." The center received "multiple reports of severe overdose," suspects adulteration with 7-hydroxymitragynine, mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 or MGM-16, states these are "more potent than morphine," and states "the product labels are misleading and do not include these drugs as ingredients."

On 30 July 2026 a death in Utah County was linked to mislabelled Buzzers-brand tablets, with MGM-15 identified. MGM-15 is a Schedule I substance under Utah law, and Utah restricted kratom products other than pure leaf in 2026, so "Legal in all 50 states" appears inaccurate.

Sold as an energy supplement. Named by a state health authority in connection with opioid overdoses and a death.

THE SAME PATTERN ACROSS THE CATEGORY

Separately, on at least one 7-OH seller's website, the product page advertises "botanical supplementation" while a help-center page on the same domain concedes the products "can be habit-forming" and that "long-term use may lead to addiction and withdrawal symptoms." Both captured 2026-08-27 and hashed. The addiction disclosure sits where a buyer will not see it before purchase.

FDA's own scientific assessment finds concentrated 7-OH is produced by chemical conversion and occurs in the leaf at roughly 0.01 percent, far below what is sold. A product created by chemical conversion, at concentrations the plant does not produce, is not botanical supplementation.

WHO IS BEING SOLD TO

In a structured-interview cohort of 1,274 treatment-seeking substance use disorder patients, childhood trauma exposure was present in 53.5 percent, and comorbid adult ADHD in 14.1 percent against roughly 2.5-5 percent in the general adult population (Addictive Behaviors, DOI 10.1016/j.addbeh.2016.10.016).

These products are sold beside the register, at gas stations and smoke shops, frequently without age verification. A representation that would mislead an ordinary consumer is a more serious matter when the foreseeable audience is this one.

REQUESTED ACTION

1. Whether marketing an opioid-active product as an energy or focus supplement is a deceptive representation under Section 5.
2. Whether "Legal in all 50 states," on a product a state poison control center has linked to overdoses and a death, is a deceptive representation.
3. Whether describing chemically converted 7-OH as "botanical supplementation" is deceptive.
4. Whether placing addiction and withdrawal disclosures away from the point of purchase is a material omission.
5. Coordination with FDA, and with the Utah and Georgia attorneys general, both of which have acted in this category.

WHAT I DO NOT CLAIM

I have assayed no product and make no claim about the contents of any specific item. The adulteration finding is the Utah Poison Control Center's. I report what the sellers' own pages say and what state authorities have published. All captures are hashed and dated.
