Introduction
        7-Hydroxymitragynine 1 is a psychoactive alkaloid found in Mitragyna speciosa (M.
speciosa) plant, a tropical evergreen tree indigenous to Southeast Asia. While the use of the
plant was once geographically limited to Southeast Asian countries such as Thailand and
Malaysia, the use of M. speciosa has since extended to the United States and other global
markets. Among the numerous alkaloids identified in M. speciosa, the indole alkaloids
mitragynine (major) and 7-hydroxymitragynine (minor) are primarily responsible for the
plant’s psychoactive effects. In its natural botanical form, 7-hydroxymitragynine makes up
less than two percent of the total alkaloid content or occurs in trace amount in M. speciosa.
However, 7-hydroxymitragynine can be synthesized from mitragynine through a one-step
step chemical reaction and it also exists as an active oxidized metabolite of mitragynine in
vivo. Despite their different origins, the chemical structures of synthetic and naturally
occurring 7-hydroxymitragynine are identical (see Figure 1). Consequently, the intrinsic
pharmacological profile, receptor affinity, and mechanism of action of 7-
hydroxymitragynine molecule remain unchanged regardless of its source. While consumers
of raw plant matrix may experience a modified or attenuated physiological effect due to the
competitive, co-occurring alkaloids inherent to M.speciosa, isolated or semi-synthetically
derived formulations deliver unattenuated, high-potency effects of the target alkaloid
directly, representing a distinct public safety profile when concentrated above the proposed
threshold.

        7-Hydroxymitragynine has opioidergic activity, sharing a similar pharmacological
profile to schedule II opioids like morphine. Preclinical data indicates that 7-
hydroxymitragynine carries a high abuse potential, with safety risks-including tolerance,
dependence, and respiratory depression, which are comparable to those of classic opioid
analgesics. The United States has recently seen an emergence of products containing 7-
hydroxymitragynine, which are often characterized by ambiguous dosages and misleading
marketing, frequently being labeled as “natural M.speciosa extracts.” While sellers
promote these products for their psychoactive and opioidergic effects of only 7-
hydroxymitragynine, evidence demonstrates these products may also contain other opioid
alkaloids, such as mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. These combinations, coupled with a lack of
regulatory oversight, pose significant safety risk to unsuspecting consumers by exposing
them to high doses of opioids. The absence of clinical evidence to support vendor health
claims is deeply concerning. Consequently, 7-hydroxymitragynine products pose
significant health risks, as essential information regarding their purity, identity, quantity and
long-term safety remain unknown.
       7-Hydroxymitragynine does not meet the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s
(FDA) safety standard for drug, dietary supplements or dietary ingredients, and has not
been proven safe or effective for any drug use. FDA has issued warning letters to
companies clarifying that 7-hydroxymitragyine is not an FDA-approved drug product and
no food additive regulation has authorized the use of 7-hydroxymitragynine in food supply.
Furthermore, FDA has warned consumers against using products labeled as 7-
hydroxymitragynine, as they have not been proven safe or effective for any use. 2 Fatal
overdoses involving 7-hydroxymitragynine have been reported, making its wide availability
and unknown safety profile a significant threat to public health, which is particularly
concerning in the midst of an opioid crisis.

component of M. speciosa. Historically, M. speciosa has been used for a variety of

once geographically limited to Southeast Asia, the marketing in the United States of 7-
hydroxymitragynine products has been aggressive and often indistinguishable from the sale
of M. speciosa (Smith et al., 2025). In recent years, 7-hydroxymitragynine has been

Product Formulation and Marketing Claims
        7-Hydroxymitragynine products are readily obtained from smoke shops, gas
stations, and online vendors in various forms, including tablets, liquid oral beverages
(shots), capsules, powder, syrup, vapes, sublingual pouch/strips, nasal spray, chewable, and
liquid extracts (Grundmann et al., 2025; Heywood et al., 2024; Hill et al., 2025; Ogozalek
2023; Sharma, et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2025). These products are commonly sold with
names such as “7 Ohmz”, “7-hydroxy”, and “7-OH” and they are presented in colorful
packages. An investigation by Hill et al (2025) identified 250 products sold between
September 2024 through February 2025, noting that chewable/sublingual tablets were the
most common formulation. Sellers claim these products can be used for general wellbeing
and increased focus. Specific marketing language for these chewable formats often
emphasizes the ease of use:
        “The chewable format is designed for convenience, allowing you to simply chew a
tablet and savor the berry flavor. This eliminates the need for mixing or preparation… The
compact size of each tablet makes them portable and perfect for on-the-go use, so you can
carry them in your bag or pocket and take them whenever needed.”

Factor 5: The scope, duration, and significance of abuse
        The abuse of 7-hydroxymitragynine is increasing and widespread. Given its
pharmacological profile as an opioid agonist with high abuse potential, the marketing of 7-
hydroxymitragynine products to consumers as botanical supplements constitutes a
significant public health risk.
        Hill et al (2025) note that 7-hydroxymitragynine, a potent opioid, presents overdose
risk and toxicity to users because its chemical manufacturing process is inconsistent and
does not adhere to good manufacturing practices. This lack of oversight may lead to
variation in doses and composition in final products. Further, 7-hydroxymitragynine is sold
as sublingual pouch/tablet, a formulation known to deliver drugs rapidly into the systemic
circulation. This rapid delivery, coupled with 7-hydroxymitragynine’s known
pharmacokinetic profile presents a significance of abuse (Hill et al., 2025; Maxwell et al.,
2021; Sharma et al., 2025).
        7-hydroxymitragynine sellers make claims of its opioidergic effects (Hill et al.,
2025). Preclinical data indicates that 7-hydroxymitragynine carries a high abuse potential,
with risks-including tolerance, dependence, and respiratory depression-comparable to those
of classic opioid analgesics. Evidence demonstrates that 7-hydroxymitragynine products
have been identified to contain other opioid alkaloids such as mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. 5

Law Enforcement Encounters
        The number of seizures for 7-hydroxymitragynine as reported in law enforcement
systems is currently limited, primarily because forensic chemists often prioritize the
identification of mitragynine, the major alkaloid in M. speciosa, rather than extending
analysis to the identification minor alkaloids such as 7-hydroxymitragynine. Furthermore,
because 7-hydroxymitragynine is not federally controlled under the CSA, additional
forensic identification may be limited. For example, some forensic laboratories may not
place emphasis on analyzing or tracking the encounters of non-controlled substances,
making it unlikely to be fully reported to forensic laboratories databases. Nonetheless,
available data from the National Forensic Laboratory Information System, 6 shows that there
has been a total of 86 reports of 7-hydroxymitagynine from 17 states (see Table 2). In
addition, according to the data report from the Department of Homeland Security (DHS)
office of field operations, seizures of M. speciosa (identified as either mitragynine or 7-
hydroxymitragynine) at ports of entry have fluctuated over the last decade, 27.18 kg in
2017, 0.52 kg in 2018, and 2,675.22 kg in 2019 (see Table 3). 7 These law enforcement data
collectively illustrate the widespread and increasing availability of products containing 7-
hydroxymitragynine within the domestic kratom drug market.

State Regulations and Controls
        Due to concerns over its abuse, several states have regulated or banned the
consumption of 7-hydroxymitragynine or M. speciosa. 10 Currently, seven states (Alabama,
Arkansas, Connecticut, Indiana, Louisiana, Vermont, and Wisconsin.) have prohibited 7-
hydroxymitragynine consumption. Additionally, some states such as Arizona, Colorado,
South Carolina, Texas have set restrictions on the limits of 7-hydroxymitragynine (not to
exceed a specified percent of total alkaloid content). Furthermore, 19 states have enacted
model legislation known as the “kratom consumer protection Act (KCPA),” which requires
that M.speciosa, mitragynine, or 7-hydroxymitragynine be manufactured safely, labeled
accurately, and distributed appropriately to protect consumers under a certain age. These
states are Arizona, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Oklahoma, Maryland,
Mississippi, Nebraska, Nevada, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South
Dakota, Texas, Utah, Virginia, and West Virginia. Other states like Illinois, New
Hampshire, North Carolina, and Tennessee have bans in some localities. 11 Of note, the
state of Mississippi has set the limit per weight basis to one percent of total alkaloid content
of 7-hydroxymitragynine or 0.5 mg per container.
Poison Control Center Data (National Poison Data System)
         The significance of 7-hydroxymitragynine abuse is demonstrated by an increasing
volume of calls to poison control centers. Reporting for 7-hydroxymitragynine was
historically limited within the national poison data system (NPDS); however, specific data
codes for this substance were recently implemented between February and May 2025.
During the initial tracking period from February 2025 to April 2025, NPDS recorded a total
of 53 case reports involving 7-hydroxymitragynine. Of these exposure calls, 37 cases
involved single-substance exposure of 7-hydroxymitragynine alone, and 24 cases were
classified as abuse involving other substances in combination with 7-hydroxymitragynine.
Among the single-substance exposure calls, 16 cases were categorized as intentional abuse,
while 13 calls involved moderate medical outcomes where the patients exhibited
pronounced and prolonged systemic symptoms. Expanded data from U.S poison centers 12
indicates a rapid escalation in reported incidents of 7-hydroxymitragynine. From January 1
through July 31, 2025, there were 165 exposure cases involving 7-hydroxymitragynine. Of
those reporting exposures to 7-hydroxymitragynine alone, 35 percent of these cases resulted
in serious health problems and 67 percent of individuals were treated at a healthcare
facility. Patients exposed to 7-hydroxymitragynine frequently exhibit a range of severe
physiological and neurological symptoms, including: gastrointestinal (nausea and

         In summary, the abuse of 7-hydroxymitragynine in the United States is fueled by its
pharmacological similarities to opioid analgesics, a lack of regulatory controls, and the
relative ease of obtaining 7-hydroxymitragynine products via smoke shops and the
Internet. 13 Furthermore, the consumption of 7-hydroxymitragynine along with other mind-
altering substances may exacerbate the potential acute and long-term hazards and risks, like
drug dependence, to the user. FDA responded to the increase in sales of 7-
hydroxymitragynine products and unsubstantiated medical claims by issuing warning letters
to companies to protect public safety. Available information from published literature and
poison control centers suggests that 7-hydroxymitragynine is used by a diverse population
for the self-treatment of various health conditions. The ingestion of 7-hydroxymitragynine,
a potent opioid, alone or co-ingestion with other substances, commonly a CNS depressant,
is of serious concern. The poison control center data and popularity of 7-
hydroxymitragynine-products collectively underscores the severity and significance of
abuse of 7-hydroxymitragynine in the United States.
Factor 6. What, if Any, Risk There Is to the Public Health
        7-hydroxymitragynine has opioidergic and addictive properties. Available
preclinical data demonstrates that 7-hydroxymitragynine has an abuse potential similar to
that of schedule I and II opioids such as heroin, morphine, and fentanyl (Hemby et al.,

FDA- Adverse Event Reporting Monitoring System (AEMS)
        Data from AEMS shows an exponential increase in incidents involving 7-
hydroxymitragynine. While only 3 cases were reported between 2023 and 2024, a DEA
query on February 27, 2026, revealed that the 2025 case count had surged to 66. As of the
most recent query on May 7, 2026, the cumulative total has reached 104 cases. The clinical
severity of these reports is exceptionally high; 97 of the 104 cases are classified as serious,
including 12 confirmed deaths. Data indicates that these adverse events are primarily
driven by drug dependence and several withdrawal syndromes.
DEA Toxicology Testing Program (DEA TOX)
        DEA TOX program, which investigates the presence of new psychoactive
substances in biological samples from drug overdose, has identified 7-hydroxymitragynine
in 85 cases since 2019. Of these, 55 were fatal and 30 were non-fatal cases. These cases
involved both males and females with a median age of 36. The average concentration of 7-
hydroxymitragynine detected in biological samples was 463.23 ng/mL. Samples often
contained other related alkaloids (mitragynine and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl) or other
drug classes, such as opioids (e.g. fentanyl), benzodiazepines (e.g. bromazolam), and
ketamine. As shown below (see Table 4), between 2020 and 2026 there has been a
significant increase in the positive identification of 7-hydroxymitragynine in fatal overdose

Conclusion of 3-Factor Analysis
       After a careful review of the scientific literature, scientific and medical evaluation
and scheduling recommendation provided by HHS, Factors 4, 5, and 6, it is evident that the
abuse of 7-hydroxymitragynine poses an imminent hazard to public safety. DEA has
considered the three factors for temporary placement of a substance under schedule I of the
CSA (21 U.S.C. 812). Available data indicate that 7-hydroxymitragynine above a specified
threshold, including its isomers, esters, ethers, salts, and salts of isomers, esters and ethers,
whenever the existence of such isomers, esters, ethers and salts is possible, poses an
imminent hazard to public safety, has no currently accepted medical use in treatment in the
United States, and lack accepted safety for use under medical supervision. Further, the
misuse of this opioid poses a high risk for overdose and life-threatening events.
