                                      Executive Summary
The Holistic Alternative Recovery Trust ("HART") was formed in 2023 to provide education to
consumers about the safety and efficacy of holistic alternative recovery solutions and to advocate
before Congress and state legislatures to promote responsible regulations of plant-based recovery
products.
We respectfully submit these comments in response to the Department of Health and Human
Services' ("HHS") Request for Information regarding the proposed threshold for the temporary
placement of 7-hydroxymitragynine ("7-OH") above a specified threshold in Schedule I of the
Controlled Substances Act.
HART supports science-based regulation of botanical products and recognizes the government's
legitimate interest in preventing the manufacture and sale of unsafe, adulterated, or deceptively
marketed products.
The question presented by this Request for Information, however, is not whether products
containing 7-OH should be regulated. Rather, HHS seeks scientific evidence identifying a
threshold above which such products present an “imminent hazard to public safety” sufficient to
justify temporary placement into Schedule I, which is reserved for those substances classified
under the U.S. Controlled Substances Act (CSA) as having:
   •   High potential for abuse
   •   No currently accepted medical use in the United States
   •   Lack of accepted safety under medical supervision

The available scientific evidence does not presently identify such a threshold.
Across pharmacology, toxicology, human clinical experience, and nationwide public-health
surveillance, the current record consistently demonstrates that risk associated with orally
consumed 7-OH products is determined by multiple interacting factors - including administered
dose, oral bioavailability, metabolism, formulation, route of administration, and concomitant
substance use - not by concentration alone. (Oral ingestion is the only known route of
consumption for 7-OH products.)

Although 7-OH is a pharmacologically active compound and, like other bioactive substances,
warrants appropriate regulatory oversight, no published pharmacological, toxicological,
epidemiological, or clinical evidence identifies either the proposed 0.050 percent concentration

threshold or the proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold as a biologically meaningful
breakpoint associated with a measurable increase in serious adverse outcomes.
That conclusion has been reached independently by experts approaching the issue from different
scientific disciplines. A review of the medical literature and multiple national surveillance
systems by Dr. Edward W. Boyer, Professor of Emergency Medicine and Medical Toxicology at
The Ohio State University, found no epidemiological signal of overdose mortality or widespread
serious toxicity attributable to orally consumed 7-OH. Dr. Kirsten E. Smith, Assistant Professor
of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences at Johns Hopkins University (2023-2026), similarly
concluded that existing evidence does not demonstrate a significant or unreasonable risk of
illness or injury, and instead supports continued research coupled with responsible regulation.
Independent reviews by Drs. Michael Levine, Dr. Jackob Moskovitz, and Dr. Andrew Monte
likewise found no evidence that currently available preclinical or surveillance data identify an
imminent public-health emergency requiring the proposed numerical thresholds. And Dr. Steven
B. Bird concluded that neither proposed threshold reflects established pharmacological risk and
recommends a finished-product threshold of five (5) mg per dosage unit.
These scientific conclusions are reinforced by real-world public-health surveillance. Federal
monitoring systems - including the National Poison Data System (NPDS), the National Drug
Early Warning System (NDEWS), and FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) - exist
specifically to detect emerging drug threats. Historically, substances presenting imminent
hazards rapidly generate recognizable patterns of poison-center reports, emergency department
presentations, published case reports, forensic findings, and overdose surveillance signals.
Despite billions of servings of commercially available oral 7-OH products consumed in the
United States, no comparable pattern has emerged. The absence of a consistent epidemiological
signal across multiple independent surveillance systems is itself scientifically significant.
Accordingly, these comments reach three principal conclusions.
First, the current scientific record does not support either of the proposed numerical
thresholds. While the available evidence confirms that 7-OH is pharmacologically active and
merits continued study and appropriate regulation, it does not identify either the proposed
concentration threshold or the proposed one-milligram threshold as scientifically established
points at which products begin presenting an imminent hazard to public safety.
Second, if HHS nevertheless determines that a numerical threshold should be
recommended as a matter of regulatory policy, that threshold should be based on actual
consumer exposure rather than an easily-gamed standard like percentage concentration.
Milligram-per-serving limits more accurately reveal pharmacological risk, are consistent with
established toxicological principles, best reflect the way Americans understand consumer
product potency, and mirror Congress's recent approach to regulating comparable products.
Finally, any threshold framework should be revised to ensure it is scientifically coherent,
administrable, and directed toward products presenting the greatest potential public-
health concern. Specifically, DEA should clarify that thresholds apply only to finished
consumer products, define "article" as a finished single-serving dosage form, eliminate the
percentage-concentration trigger, and increase the finished-product threshold from 1 mg to 5 mg
per dosage unit. These revisions would preserve DEA's ability to address genuinely high-potency

I. The Scientific Record Does Not Support the Proposed Thresholds
The Request for Information poses a straightforward scientific question: does the available
evidence identify a concentration or dosage threshold above which products containing 7-
hydroxymitragynine ("7-OH") present an imminent hazard to the public safety?
The current scientific record does not answer that question in the affirmative.
The available evidence demonstrates that 7-OH is a pharmacologically active compound that
warrants continued scientific study and appropriate regulatory oversight. However, it does not
identify either the proposed 0.050 percent concentration threshold or the proposed one-
milligram-per-article threshold as scientifically established breakpoints associated with a
measurable increase in serious adverse outcomes. Rather, the evidence consistently demonstrates
that risk is determined by multiple interacting pharmacological, toxicological, and clinical
variables that cannot be reduced to concentration alone.
Importantly, this conclusion is not derived from a single study, researcher, or even discipline.
Independent experts in pharmacology, medical toxicology, emergency medicine, behavioral
science, poison surveillance, and clinical research - including Drs. Steven Bird, Edward Boyer,
Kirsten Smith, Michael Levine, Jackob Moskovitz, and Andrew Monte - reviewed different
portions of the available evidence and nevertheless reached remarkably consistent conclusions.
Although they approached the issue from the perspective of different scientific disciplines, none
identified evidence supporting DEA's proposed numerical thresholds as biologically meaningful
indicators of imminent public-health risk.
A. Pharmacology Does Not Identify a Scientifically Meaningful Breakpoint
The fact that 7-OH exhibits pharmacological activity does not itself establish an appropriate
regulatory threshold. Modern pharmacology distinguishes among pharmacological potency,
systemic exposure, and actual clinical risk. While 7-OH functions as a partial agonist at the μ-
opioid receptor, receptor binding alone does not predict toxicity. Instead, clinical effects depend
upon the amount of drug that ultimately reaches systemic circulation and target tissues following
oral administration.
The pharmacology of 7-OH further illustrates why receptor affinity alone is an incomplete
measure of public-health risk. Although 7-OH binds with high affinity to the μ-opioid receptor, it
is a G protein-biased partial agonist that exhibits relatively low intrinsic efficacy, more closely
resembling buprenorphine than full μ-opioid agonists. (Kruegel, 2016; Obeng, 2021; Todd,
2020; Varadi, 2016; WHO, 2021). As a partial agonist, 7-OH does not fully activate the receptor
even when receptor occupancy increases, producing a pharmacological ceiling on opioid-like
effects that distinguishes it from conventional opioids.
This distinction is particularly important because public discussions of 7-OH frequently conflate
potency with intrinsic efficacy. While 7-OH demonstrates higher receptor potency than morphine
in certain laboratory assays involving guinea pigs (and not humans), potency reflects the amount
of drug required to produce a given effect—not the maximum effect the drug is capable of
producing. Because 7-OH is a partial agonist with comparatively low intrinsic efficacy,


                                                 3
statements that it is "more potent than morphine" do not establish that it produces morphine-like
toxicity or overdose risk in humans.
Consistent with this pharmacological profile, experimental studies further demonstrate that 7-OH
produces substantially reduced β-arrestin-2 recruitment compared with conventional opioid
agonists. This signaling pathway has been associated with respiratory depression and other dose-
limiting adverse effects of classical opioids. (Todd, 2020; Samways, 2024). More importantly,
federally funded research conducted at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center identified that
7-OH does not induce respiratory depression (DoD award number W81XWH-17-1-0256, “Safer
nonaddicting and nonabusable analgesics....;” Majumda).
While β-arrestin signaling alone cannot predict clinical outcomes, these findings provide a
biologically plausible explanation for why the available preclinical, clinical, and epidemiological
evidence has not demonstrated the pattern of severe respiratory toxicity typically associated with
full μ-opioid agonists.
These mechanistic differences are further amplified by 7-OH's pharmacokinetics. Orally
administered 7-OH exhibits extremely poor oral bioavailability - approximately 2.7 percent -
meaning that only a small fraction of an ingested dose survives first-pass metabolism and reaches
systemic circulation. (Chiang et al., 2025). Accordingly, receptor potency analyses tend to
substantially overstate exposure experienced following oral consumption of commercially
available products.
As Dr. Steven B. Bird explains in his independent toxicological review, a finished dosage unit
containing up to 5 mg of 7-OH would be expected to produce plasma concentrations
approximately fifteen times lower than those observed in published human kratom studies that
reported no signal of serious toxicity or respiratory depression. These findings undergird Dr.
Bird’s recommendation of a 5mg dosage limit per article, and the aforementioned
pharmacokinetic characteristics substantially limit systemic exposure and refute the scientific
premise that either the proposed concentration threshold or the proposed one-milligram limit
corresponds to a meaningful increase in pharmacological risk.
Dr. Edward Boyer's 2025 review reached the same conclusion from a different perspective.
Evaluating FDA's 2025 scientific assessment, Dr. Boyer explained that the agency places undue
emphasis on receptor affinity while giving insufficient consideration to exposure-response
relationships – the very principle that FDA has long recognized as central to evaluating drug
safety. As FDA's own guidance explains, "exposure-response information is at the heart of any
determination of safety." In Dr. Boyer's view, pharmacological potency, standing alone, is an
inadequate predictor of toxicity because it fails to account for the pharmacokinetic factors that
determine actual human exposure.
Dr. Boyer noted that the compound exhibits poor oral bioavailability, short residence time within
the body, and limited penetration into the central nervous system. Collectively, these
characteristics substantially reduce systemic exposure following oral consumption and help
explain why the expected epidemiological signal associated with highly toxic opioid agonists has
not emerged despite widespread consumer use.
These principles carry important regulatory implications. Products containing identical
concentrations of 7-OH may produce materially different systemic exposures depending upon


                                                 4
formulation, dosage form, absorption characteristics, metabolism, and route of administration.
Conversely, products with substantially different concentration percentages may expose
consumers to similar amounts of active compound. Put simply, concentration is not synonymous
with exposure, and exposure – not nominal concentration – is the relevant determinant of
pharmacological risk.
For this reason, neither Dr. Bird nor Dr. Boyer identifies the proposed 0.050 percent
concentration threshold or the proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold as scientifically
meaningful pharmacological breakpoints. Instead, both conclude that actual consumer exposure
provides the more scientifically appropriate framework for evaluating risk.
Finally, and perhaps most consequentially, the relative innocuousness of 7-OH was seemingly
confirmed by FDA-affiliated researchers, who led a 2026 randomized control trial on kratom in
which no serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred among human subjects. Investigators
administered increasing doses of kratom powder at levels of 1.5% mitragynine, up to 12,000mg
(12g). Since the human liver converts approximately 25% of mitragynine into 7-OH, study
participants received up to 45mg of 7-OH (12,000 * .015 *.25 – a higher dosage than most
consumers use, according to industry surveys. Quoting directly from the recently-published
study: “No deaths or serious adverse events occurred.”
Accordingly, the available pharmacological evidence does not establish that products abruptly
transition from presenting ordinary pharmacological activity to constituting an imminent hazard
to public safety at either of DEA's proposed numerical thresholds. Rather, the evidence
demonstrates that pharmacological risk exists along a continuum governed by exposure, dose,
formulation, metabolism, and route of administration. To the extent HHS ultimately recommends
a numerical threshold as a matter of regulatory policy, that threshold should therefore be
understood as a policy judgment informed by science – not a value uniquely dictated by the
current scientific record.
B. Preclinical Toxicology Does Not Identify an Imminent Hazard or Support the Proposed
Thresholds
The available preclinical toxicology likewise does not identify a scientifically meaningful
concentration or dosage threshold supporting DEA's proposed framework.
Animal and in vitro studies are an essential component of safety assessment because they help
identify potential toxicological concerns before widespread human exposure occurs. At the same
time, toxicology has long recognized that preclinical findings must ultimately be interpreted
alongside pharmacokinetics, clinical experience, and real-world epidemiological data. Animal
models are designed to identify potential hazards - not to establish the precise regulatory
thresholds at which products present an imminent public-health emergency for humans.
The currently available preclinical evidence regarding orally administered 7-OH does not
demonstrate the type of toxicological profile ordinarily associated with emergency regulatory
intervention. Independent reviews conducted by Dr. Michael Levine, Professor of Emergency
Medicine and Co-Chief of the Division of Medical Toxicology at UCLA, and Dr. Jackob
Moskovitz, Associate Professor of Pharmacology and Toxicology at the University of Kansas
School of Pharmacy, reached substantially similar conclusions despite evaluating different
aspects of the available evidence. Both concluded that existing preclinical studies support


                                                5
continued scientific investigation but do not identify evidence of serious toxicity sufficient to
characterize currently marketed oral 7-OH products as presenting an imminent public-health
hazard.
Dr. Levine reviewed both a rodent toxicity study and a canine safety study, focusing specifically
on the types of adverse effects that would ordinarily be expected from a compound acting at the
μ-opioid receptor - particularly central nervous system depression and respiratory depression.
His review found that no animals exhibited serious clinical toxicity during the course of either
study. While minor findings such as transient drooling and gastrointestinal abnormalities were
observed in some animals, similar findings also occurred in control animals, making a causal
relationship to 7-OH difficult to establish. Dr. Levine ultimately concluded that the available
preclinical evidence demonstrated no serious toxicological signal and supported further
investigation through appropriately designed human studies rather than suggesting an imminent
safety concern.
Dr. Moskovitz independently evaluated the available in vitro, rodent, and canine studies and
reached remarkably similar conclusions. His review found that 7-OH demonstrated no clinically
significant evidence of cardiac, hepatic, or renal toxicity in cell-based assays and that no
meaningful in-life toxicity was observed in the animal models. Indeed, he concluded that the
available evidence suggests that the safety profile of 7-OH is comparable to - or in certain
respects more favorable than - that of the related naturally occurring kratom alkaloid
mitragynine, which will remain legal under this scheduling order. Based upon the totality of the
available laboratory and animal evidence, Dr. Moskovitz concluded that the data do not support
the conclusion that 7-OH presents a significant or serious safety concern.
These conclusions are notable not because they establish that 7-OH is entirely without risk – no
pharmacologically active compound is – but because they demonstrate what the current evidence
does not show: a toxicological breakpoint corresponding to either DEA's proposed 0.050 percent
concentration threshold or its proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold. Nor does it
demonstrate the severe respiratory depression, persistent neurological impairment, target-organ
toxicity, or other serious adverse effects that ordinarily precede emergency scheduling actions
involving newly emerging substances.
Dr. Steven Bird likewise observed that the available pharmacological and toxicological data do
not support the proposition that products containing up to 5 mg of orally administered 7-OH
present an imminent public-health hazard. Instead, his analysis concludes that a 5 mg finished-
product threshold remains conservative when considered alongside the compound's limited oral
bioavailability and the absence of corresponding evidence of serious toxicity in the available
preclinical literature.
Taken together, the available animal, in vitro, and pharmacokinetic evidence supports a
consistent conclusion. The existing preclinical record identifies the need for continued research -
as would be expected for any relatively new bioactive compound - but it does not identify either
of DEA's proposed numerical thresholds as scientifically established toxicological breakpoints.
Instead, the evidence supports continued investigation, appropriate manufacturing controls, and
ongoing surveillance while human clinical data continue to develop.
C. Human Experience Does Not Demonstrate Widespread Serious Harm or Identify a
Scientifically Meaningful Threshold


                                                  6
Ultimately, the most probative evidence of public-health risk is real-world human experience.
While pharmacological and preclinical studies provide important information regarding
biological activity and potential toxicity, they cannot substitute for evidence demonstrating how
a substance actually performs following widespread human use. Here, the available human
evidence weighs strongly against the conclusion that 7-OH products present an imminent hazard
to public safety or that either of DEA's proposed numerical thresholds corresponds to a
measurable increase in serious adverse outcomes.
This conclusion is particularly significant because commercially available 7-OH products have
now been consumed on an extraordinary scale. By conservative estimates, more than one million
Americans have consumed billions of servings of these products, nearly all in dosages vastly
exceeding the proposed 1 mg threshold. Had currently marketed oral 7-OH products been
producing the type of severe toxicity ordinarily associated with an emerging public-health crisis,
one would reasonably expect to observe a corresponding increase in published case reports,
poison-center alerts, emergency department presentations, overdose investigations, and forensic
findings. That pattern has not emerged.
To HART's knowledge, after reviewing published case reports, FDA adverse-event reports,
poison-center data, and the expert analyses submitted in this proceeding, there is no confirmed
fatality in which orally consumed 7-OH ingested in isolation has been established as the cause of
death - despite billions of servings consumed. Fatalities reported in surveillance systems almost
invariably involve significant polysubstance exposure (often alongside heroin, fentanyl, or other
substances known to cause death), or other substantial confounding factors, and several cases
cited in public discussions predate the commercial introduction of modern 7-OH products
altogether. Although the absence of confirmed isolated fatalities does not by itself establish
safety, it is highly probative when considered alongside the compound's pharmacology, the
available toxicological evidence, and the absence of a broader epidemiological signal.
Dr. Edward W. Boyer’s comprehensive review of the published literature, poison-center
surveillance, adverse-event reports, forensic data, and federal monitoring systems found no
published case reports documenting acute poisoning or overdose deaths directly attributable to 7-
OH products.
Medical toxicologists have historically identified emerging public-health threats with remarkable
speed, including outbreaks involving synthetic cannabinoids and tianeptine. According to Dr.
Boyer, when a substance is producing significant overdose morbidity or mortality, multiple cases
rapidly generate recognizable epidemiological signals. In the case of 7-OH, however, no such
signal has emerged.
Dr. Boyer further concluded that the absence of overdose reports is not simply the product of
underreporting or unfamiliarity with the compound. Rather, it reflects the fact that nationwide
surveillance systems specifically designed to detect emerging toxicological threats have not
identified evidence of widespread serious harm associated with 7-OH. As he explains, "multiple
overdoses generate signals," yet no comparable signal has appeared despite years of increasing
consumer exposure. This observation is particularly significant because the same surveillance
infrastructure has successfully identified numerous emerging substances posing genuine public-
health concerns long before they became widely recognized.

Dr. Kirsten E. Smith, one of the nation's leading researchers on kratom and its alkaloids,
independently reached substantially similar conclusions after reviewing preclinical evidence,

demonstrate widespread or clinically significant respiratory depression associated with orally
consumed 7-OH. Although very high experimental doses in animal models – administered, in at
least one case, intravenously – have produced transient respiratory effects, these findings have
not translated into a recognizable pattern of respiratory failure or fatal overdose among human
consumers using commercially available oral products.
Neither expert suggests that 7-OH is entirely without risk. To the contrary, both acknowledge
that continued scientific investigation remains appropriate and that products containing 7-OH
should be manufactured, labeled, and marketed responsibly. Their conclusions instead reflect a
more limited – but critically important – scientific proposition: the currently available human
evidence does not identify either DEA's proposed 0.050 percent concentration threshold or its
proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold as scientifically meaningful points at which
products begin presenting an imminent hazard to public safety.
This distinction is critical because the question before HHS is not whether products containing 7-
OH should be subject to appropriate regulatory safeguards. Rather, it is whether the available
scientific evidence supports identifying a specific numerical threshold that poses an imminent
threat to public health. At this time, the published literature, clinical experience, adverse-event
data, and observational research do not support that conclusion.
Accordingly, the existing human evidence supports continued surveillance, additional clinical
research, appropriate manufacturing standards, and responsible product regulation. It does not
support the conclusion that either of the proposed numerical thresholds is uniquely established
by the current scientific record.
D. Public Health Surveillance Does Not Demonstrate an Emerging Public Health
Emergency
Independent public-health surveillance provides perhaps the strongest test of whether a substance
presents an imminent hazard to public safety. Unlike preclinical research or isolated case reports,


                                                8
surveillance systems evaluate population-level outcomes across millions of real-world exposures.
They are specifically designed to identify emerging patterns of overdose, poisoning,
hospitalization, and death that may warrant rapid public-health or regulatory intervention.
Historically, substances posing genuine and immediate public-health threats generate
recognizable epidemiological signals shortly after widespread availability. Synthetic
cannabinoids, synthetic cathinones, fentanyl analogues, and tianeptine all produced significant
increases in poison-center calls, emergency department presentations, forensic toxicology
findings, published case reports, and overdose fatalities that were rapidly detected through
national surveillance systems. Those signals formed an important part of the scientific basis for
subsequent regulatory action.
The available evidence concerning commercially available 7-OH products presents a markedly
different picture.
As Dr. Edward Boyer explained, the United States maintains multiple complementary
surveillance systems capable of identifying emerging toxicological threats, including the
National Poison Data System ("NPDS"), DEA TOX, the National Drug Early Warning System
("NDEWS"), FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System ("FAERS"), published medical case
reports, and forensic toxicology reporting. Collectively, these systems monitor poison-center
consultations, emergency department encounters, toxicological investigations, adverse-event
reporting, overdose deaths, and trends in illicit drug markets. According to Dr. Boyer's review,
none of these independent surveillance systems has identified a pattern of widespread serious
toxicity attributable to 7-OH products.
This absence of a consistent epidemiological signal is scientifically significant. Surveillance
systems are inherently imperfect, and isolated adverse events may go unreported. However, the
purpose of these systems is not to capture every individual incident; rather, they are designed to
detect meaningful population-level trends. When a substance produces substantial morbidity or
mortality across large numbers of users, those trends ordinarily become visible across multiple
independent datasets. As Dr. Boyer observed, there is a “lack of [a] public health signal of
adverse effects from 7OH.” The failure of multiple surveillance systems to identify such a signal
despite extensive consumer exposure weighs against the conclusion that currently marketed oral
7-OH products present an imminent public-health emergency.
Dr. Kirsten Smith independently reviewed many of the same surveillance datasets and reached
substantially similar conclusions. Her assessment found no evidence of widespread illness,
injury, or overdose sufficient to support the conclusion that oral 7-OH products present a
significant or unreasonable risk to the public. Although adverse events have been reported - as
would be expected with virtually any pharmacologically active product - the available data do
not reveal the type of consistent, reproducible pattern ordinarily associated with an emerging
national health crisis. Instead, the existing evidence supports continued monitoring while
additional clinical and epidemiological data are developed.
Dr. Andrew Monte, Professor of Emergency Medicine and Medical Toxicology at the University
of Colorado School of Medicine and a principal investigator with the RADARS® System,
likewise concluded that the available surveillance evidence does not suggest an unreasonable risk
to public health. Dr. Monte's review is particularly informative because RADARS® was
specifically developed to identify emerging patterns of misuse, abuse, diversion, overdose, and


                                                9
other adverse outcomes associated with prescription and non-prescription substances. Based on
the currently available evidence, Dr. Monte concluded that continued surveillance remains
appropriate but that existing data do not support the conclusion that commercially available 7-
OH products are producing a level of harm warranting the proposed numerical thresholds.
These conclusions are further reinforced by the scale of consumer exposure. According to
independent market analyses, more than one million Americans have consumed, per industry
estimates, billions of commercially available 7-OH products since their introduction to the
marketplace in 2023. While widespread utilization alone cannot establish safety, it substantially
increases the likelihood that meaningful safety concerns would become apparent through the
very surveillance systems designed to detect them. The absence of a consistent signal across
multiple independent surveillance programs after such extensive exposure is therefore an
important component of the overall scientific record.
Importantly, this does not mean that surveillance should cease or that regulation is unnecessary.
On the contrary, continued monitoring through NPDS, NDEWS, DEA TOX, RADARS®,
FAERS, and other established public-health systems remains essential as consumer use evolves
and additional research becomes available. Surveillance is most valuable when it informs
evidence-based regulatory decision-making over time rather than being viewed as a one-time
exercise.
Taken together, the pharmacological evidence, preclinical toxicology, human clinical experience,
and nationwide surveillance data lead to the same conclusion. The current scientific record
supports continued research, ongoing surveillance, responsible manufacturing standards, and
appropriate regulatory oversight. It does not identify either the proposed 0.050 percent
concentration threshold or the proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold as scientifically
established points at which products begin presenting an imminent hazard to public safety.
Accordingly, if HHS determines that numerical thresholds remain appropriate as a matter of
regulatory policy, those thresholds should be understood as policy judgments informed by the
available science - not as values uniquely dictated by the current scientific record. The following
recommendations in Part III of this response are offered with that distinction in mind.

II. Emergency Scheduling Should Be Reserved for Demonstrable Public Health
Emergencies

Congress vested the temporary scheduling authority in the Controlled Substances Act to enable
the federal government to respond rapidly to extraordinary and emergent threats to public health.
By design, it is an exceptional regulatory mechanism that permits expedited action before the
completion of the ordinary rulemaking process. That authority is therefore most appropriately
reserved for circumstances in which the available scientific evidence demonstrates an imminent
hazard requiring immediate intervention.

The scientific record summarized above does not present that circumstance.

The evidence confirms that 7-OH is a pharmacologically active compound that warrants
continued study and appropriate regulatory oversight. However, across pharmacology,
toxicology, human experience, and nationwide surveillance, the current record does not identify


                                                10
the hallmarks of an emerging public-health emergency. Independent experts reviewing different
aspects of the available evidence consistently conclude that the proposed numerical thresholds
are not scientifically established breakpoints associated with a measurable increase in serious
harm. Likewise, the national surveillance systems specifically designed to detect emerging drug
threats have not identified the type of consistent epidemiological signal that has historically
preceded emergency regulatory action against newly emerging substances.

The absence of evidence supporting emergency scheduling does not imply that regulation is
unnecessary. Agencies routinely regulate products presenting varying degrees of risk through
ordinary administrative processes informed by evolving scientific evidence. Temporary
scheduling, however, occupies a different place within the statutory framework. Because it
authorizes immediate and sweeping regulatory consequences, it should be reserved for situations
in which the scientific record demonstrates an imminent threat that cannot reasonably await the
ordinary deliberative process.

Nothing in these comments should be understood as discouraging HHS from recommending
appropriate safeguards for products containing 7-OH. To the contrary, HART supports
reasonable manufacturing standards, age restrictions, product testing, labeling requirements, and
continued surveillance to ensure consumer safety. The question presented by this Request for
Information, however, is considerably narrower: whether the current scientific evidence supports
the extraordinary remedy of temporary Schedule I placement through DEA's emergency
scheduling authority.

Based on the record presently before the Agency, it does not.

Accordingly, if HHS concludes that additional federal controls are warranted, it should
recommend that DEA proceed through the ordinary scheduling process established by the
Controlled Substances Act. That process is specifically designed to permit comprehensive
scientific evaluation, robust public participation, and reasoned administrative decision-making
before imposing the substantial legal consequences associated with permanent scheduling. By
contrast, temporary scheduling is an extraordinary authority intended to address imminent
hazards to public safety. The current scientific record does not demonstrate the type of
immediate public-health emergency that justifies bypassing the ordinary scheduling process
Congress established.

III. Improving the Proposed Threshold Framework
Although the scientific record discussed above does not support either the proposed 0.050
percent concentration threshold or the proposed one-milligram-per-article threshold as
scientifically established indicators of an imminent hazard to public safety, HART recognizes
that HHS and DEA may conclude, as a matter of regulatory policy, that numerical thresholds are
appropriate to distinguish between products presenting materially different levels of potential
risk.
If so, those thresholds should be structured in a manner that reflects established toxicological
principles, can be implemented consistently by regulated manufacturers, and directs enforcement
resources toward products presenting the greatest potential public-health concern. Several



                                               11
targeted revisions would substantially improve the proposed framework while remaining faithful
to the scientific record.
A. Clarify That Thresholds Apply Only to Finished Consumer Products
The proposed rule repeatedly refers to an "article" containing more than one milligram of 7-OH
but does not define that term with sufficient precision. This ambiguity creates unnecessary
uncertainty for manufacturers, laboratories, distributors, and enforcement personnel.
For purposes of temporary scheduling, DEA should expressly define an "article" as a finished
consumer dosage unit intended for retail sale or consumption, such as a tablet, capsule, beverage,
gummy, or other single-serving product.
Applying thresholds to intermediate manufacturing materials, bulk ingredients, or works-in-
progress would create significant compliance difficulties without advancing the Agency's stated
public-health objectives. Concentrated intermediate materials are routinely used during lawful
manufacturing processes before dilution into finished products. Regulating those materials under
a finished-product threshold could inadvertently criminalize ordinary manufacturing practices
that never result in consumer exposure.
Limiting the threshold to finished consumer products would more accurately align regulatory
oversight with actual public health risk while providing regulated entities with a clear and
administrable compliance standard.
B. Eliminate the Percentage Concentration Threshold
Among the two proposed numerical thresholds, the percentage concentration limit is the least
scientifically defensible.
As discussed in Part I, pharmacological risk is determined by systemic exposure, not
concentration expressed as a percentage of product weight. Two products containing identical
concentration percentages may produce dramatically different consumer exposures depending
upon serving size, dosage form, oral bioavailability, and formulation. Conversely, products with
substantially different concentration percentages may deliver nearly identical amounts of active
compound.
Dr. Steven Bird specifically concludes that concentration-based regulation does not accurately
reflect pharmacological risk because concentration alone fails to measure the quantity of 7-OH
actually consumed. Dr. Edward Boyer likewise emphasizes that toxicological risk depends upon
exposure-response relationships rather than receptor affinity or nominal concentration.
These conclusions are consistent with longstanding toxicological principles. Hazard is not
determined simply by the proportion of active ingredient within a product, but by the amount
ultimately absorbed by the consumer under real-world conditions of use.
For these reasons, HHS should recommend that DEA eliminate the proposed 0.050 percent
concentration threshold and instead regulate finished products using a single exposure-based
standard.
C. Increase the Finished-Product Threshold to 5 mg Per Dosage Unit




                                               12
If HHS concludes that a finished-product threshold remains appropriate, the scientific record
supports increasing that threshold from one milligram to five milligrams per finished dosage
unit.

I.   Pharmacokinetic data of in vivo 7-OH formation from mitragynine

The serum/blood concentrations and duration of 7-OH are dictated by the rate at which MTG is
metabolized. In humans, MTG is converted into 7-OH primarily through CYP3A4-mediated
metabolism in the liver. While CYP3A4 is the major driver, enzymes such as CYP2C19 and
CYP2D6 contribute to this conversion to a lesser extent.

Both the formation and clearance of 7-OH are CYP3A-dependent. Ketoconazole (a CYP3A
inhibitor) increased systemic exposure to 7-OH by 130% in rats and increased its
pharmacodynamic potency 4- to 10-fold, confirming that CYP3A is the principal clearance
pathway for 7-OH itself, not just its formation from MTG. In humans, women generally exhibit
higher CYP3A4 activity, which would lead to lower 7-OH concentrations compared to men. 7-OH
undergoes further oxidative metabolism to at least two identified products:

     •   Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl — formed via a CYP3A-mediated skeletal rearrangement. This
         metabolite is a multifunctional μ-agonist/δ-κ antagonist with >2-fold μ-opioid receptor-
         biased agonism relative to its precursors, and it exhibits reinforcing properties and
         gastrointestinal (GI) transit inhibition (Chakraborty 2021; Avula 2026).
     •   3-Dehydromitragynine — formed through a non-CYP oxidation pathway. This metabolite
         is also generated when 7-OH degrades under simulated gastric conditions (up to 27%
         degradation in simulated gastric fluid) (Manda 2014; Chakraborty 2021; Avula 2026).

The most detailed human data regarding 7-OH come from the study by Huestis et al. (Huestis
MA. Ther Drug Monit 2026;00:1–11), which characterized plasma PK of both mitragynine and 7-
OH-MTG after oral kratom extract (39.5% mitragynine) at escalating doses.

     •   After single doses of 9.9, 29.6, and 59.2 mg mitragynine, mean 7-OH Cmax values were
         6.8, 13.7, and 30.5 ng/mL, respectively, with a median Tmax of 1.3–1.7 h.
     •   After 15 daily doses at the same escalating levels, mean 7-OH Cmax values were 5.4, 16.6,
         and 33.9 ng/mL with Tmax of 1.7–2.3 hours.
     •   Cmax and AUC increased approximately dose-proportionally (less than 1.4 x greater than
         expected based on strict dose proportionality).
     •   Higher 7-OH to mitragynine ratios were observed after multiple doses compared to single
         doses, and at lower compared to higher doses, suggesting possible CYP3A enzyme
         saturation at higher doses.

Because 7-OH formation is rate-limited, its elimination half-life when formed as a metabolite is
parallel to that of MTG, approximately 4.7 to 24.7 hours in human subjects depending on the study
and dose.

II. Pharmacokinetic data regarding 7-OH when used alone

After exhaustive literature review, I could find no data regarding the PK (or pharmacodynamics)
of 7-OH when ingested alone in humans. In their absence, I have used fundamental PK principles
to estimate human 7-OH concentrations.

Several factors suggest that absolute oral bioavailability of 7-OH is likely modest:
   •   Gastric instability: 7-OH degrades up to 27% in simulated gastric fluid, with approximately
       23% converting back to mitragynine and additional degradation to 3-dehydromitragynine.
       Only 6% degradation occurs in simulated intestinal fluid (Manda 2014).
   •   Rapid hepatic metabolism: 7-OH has a microsomal half-life of only 24 minutes in human
       liver microsomes, indicating extensive first-pass metabolism (Manda 2014).
   •   CYP3A-dependent clearance.

PK studies in rodents have been performed. Chiang et al. (Chiang 2025), performed oral and IV
PK studies of 7-OH in male rats. The volume of distribution was 2.7 ± 0.4 L/kg and the clearance
was 4.0 ± 0.3 L/h/kg after IV administration. Notably, the volume of distribution of MTG in humans
and rodents show 97% agreement. It is expected that the volume of distribution in humans would
also be low. After 5 mg/kg oral dosing, a Cmax of 28.5 ± 5.0 ng/ml and Tmax of 0.3 ± 0.1 h were
observed. Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI), the metabolite of 7-OH that is a more potent μ-
opioid agonist than 7-OH, had a parent-to-metabolite ratio for MGPI following IV 7-OH of 0.5 ±
0.1%, indicating very limited systemic exposure to MGPI.

Further PK data from rats are also informative. 7-OH exhibits >90% plasma protein binding by
equilibrium dialysis, similar to mitragynine (Manda 2014). Such high binding limits the free fraction
available for tissue distribution and receptor engagement. Furthermore, Yusof et al. (Yusof 2019)
determined that 7-OH has lower blood-brain-barrier (BBB) permeability than mitragynine (that is,
7-OH does not as readily cross into the brain); 7-OH shows a tendency toward P-gp-mediated
efflux (efflux ratio 1.39); and less than 10% of unbound 7-OH in plasma crosses the BBB (Kp,uu
< 0.1), indicating extensive efflux.

So, despite the limitations of data in humans, all available evidence supports my conclusions.

IV. The 7-OH serum/blood concentration at or above which a health risk (namely,
    respiratory depression) may occur

Given 7-OH’s ability to bind to the μ-opiate receptor, the greatest concern regarding overdose of
7-OH is respiratory depression.

There is no scientifically established serum/blood concentration of 7-OH at or above which
respiratory depression is likely to occur. Aside from the lack of clinical data, the most compelling
reason for this lack of respiratory effect threshold is that 7-OH exhibits atypical opioid
pharmacology. 7-OH is a potent partial agonist at the -opioid receptor. Unlike full agonists (such
as morphine or fentanyl), partial agonists often demonstrate a “ceiling effect” for respiratory
depression in preclinical models. While 7-OH has a much higher binding affinity and potency at
the -opioid receptor than mitragynine, its G-protein-biased signaling complicates setting a linear
concentration-to-risk threshold. These facts (and others), make the likelihood of respiratory
depression less likely with 7-OH.

Given this lack of clinical data on respiratory effects, perhaps the best and most voluminous data
on the safety of 7-OH comes from a review of Poison Center and other data (e.g., DEA) and the
manufacturers of 7-OH.

Reports of 7-OH toxicity and deaths potentially linked to 7-OH in the realm of medicine and
medical toxicology are generally rare. However, the real-world use of 7-OH products (in which the
most common 7-OH dose sold in the U.S. is the 50 mg tablet) is tremendous, with more than
93,381,000 tablets sold in the U.S. (which is likely an underestimate of the true consumption of
7-OH). Reconciling the large use of 7-OH products with the relative dearth of reports of 7-OH
toxicity resulting in significant adverse health effects can only lead to the conclusion that 7-OH is
relatively safe, even at large doses such as 50 mg or more.

While it is hard to prove a negative, I believe that data related to total production of 7-OH products
provides a compelling argument for 7-OH’s safety when used at reasonable doses.

V. My answers to the questions posed to me

Q1.    Whether the available human clinical data support DEA publication of a temporary order
       scheduling an article containing greater than 1 mg of 7-OH in schedule I of the Controlled
       Substances Act.

Available human and animal studies do not support a 7-OH threshold/dose that low. Available
human and animal studies support establishment of a 7-OH threshold/dose higher than 1 mg per
article. Evidence for this opinion is found in the human PK data by Huestis et al. which showed
that 7-OH Cmax from mitragynine doses up to 59.2 mg were well-tolerated. Additionally, we have
real-world use of millions of 50 mg tablets without proportionate adverse events. It is also worth
mentioning that I could find no rationale or justification for DEA’s 1 mg limit recommendation.

Q2.    Whether the DEA’s proposed 1 mg-per-article threshold is scientifically justified in light of
       available human PK, safety, and tolerability data.

It is my opinion that the 1 mg-per-article threshold is overly conservative based upon pre-clinical
and clinical science. Such a limit also lacks validity given what is known about 7-OH use in the
United States.

Q3.    Whether a higher threshold, including one informed by observed in vivo conversion of
       mitragynine to 7-OH, is scientifically more appropriate.

It is my opinion that a 5 mg per article dose is safe and does not present an imminent hazard to
the public safety. Support for this opinion is found in the Huestis human PK studies, in which a
high 7-OH Cmax of 30.5 ng/mL was found after mitragynine dosing. Furthermore, the partial
agonist/ceiling effect of 7-OH lends another pharmacodynamic level of safety to the chemical. In
fact, given all of the data that I have reviewed, it would also be my opinion that a 10 mg dose is
safe.

Q4.    Whether the DEA’s proposal for both a “greater than 0.050 percentage by weight/weight,
       weight/volume, or volume/volume” per article concentration standard and a “greater than
       1.00 milligram” per article standard creates scientific and regulatory ambiguity, and
       whether the 0.050% standard should be deleted in favor of a single threshold expressed
       in milligrams per article.

It is my opinion that the DEA’s use of both a 0.050% concentration standard and a 1 mg-per-
article standard is not necessary and is unduly complicated. It is not the concentration
(weight/weight) that is important, but rather the milligrams of 7-OH per dose. Requiring just a
milligram-based standard allows for much clearer dosing guidance across products of varying
size and formulation.

Q5.    Any assumptions, methodological limitations, or safety caveats that should accompany
       your opinion.

My opinions are based upon available data. Ideally, PK studies of 7-OH would be performed.
Such studies would eliminate some of the extrapolations being relied upon for my opinion (e.g.,
7-OH metabolite data from Huestis; rodent PK data; and protein binding estimates). Due to the
lack of human PK data for 7-OH, the most appropriate safe dosage is deemed to be 5 mg per
article.
